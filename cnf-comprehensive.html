Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF)

Artificial Intelligence — Logical Agents Chapter

What is CNF?

A sentence in propositional logic is in Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF) when it is expressed as a conjunction of clauses, where each clause is a disjunction of literals.

CNF Structure:
C₁ ∧ C₂ ∧ ... ∧ Cₙ
A CNF sentence is a conjunction (AND) of n clauses
Each Clause Cᵢ:
l₁ ∨ l₂ ∨ ... ∨ lₘ
Each clause is a disjunction (OR) of m literals
Each Literal lᵢ:
A propositional symbol or its negation (¬A)
A literal is an atomic proposition or its negation
Example: CNF Structure
(A ∨ ¬B ∨ C) ∧ (¬A ∨ B) ∧ (¬C)

This CNF has 3 clauses: C₁ = (A ∨ ¬B ∨ C), C₂ = (¬A ∨ B), C₃ = (¬C)

Key Rule: In CNF, negation (¬) may ONLY appear directly in front of a propositional symbol. You cannot have ¬(A ∧ B) or ¬(A ∨ B) in CNF.

Why Do We Need CNF?

Resolution Requires It

The resolution inference rule only applies to clauses (disjunctions of literals). To use resolution, the KB must be in CNF.

Complete Inference

Every sentence in propositional logic is logically equivalent to a CNF sentence. So converting to CNF loses no information.

Standard Form

CNF provides a canonical representation that makes automated reasoning algorithms practical and uniform.

Foundation for Algorithms

DPLL, WalkSAT, and resolution all operate on CNF. It is the input format for satisfiability solvers.

Key Theorem:
Every propositional sentence is logically equivalent to some CNF sentence
This guarantees we can always convert to CNF without loss

4-Step CNF Conversion Procedure

Any propositional sentence can be converted to CNF using these four steps:

Step 1: Eliminate Biconditionals (↔)

Replace (α ↔ β) with (α → β) ∧ (β → α)

α ↔ β ≡ (α → β) ∧ (β → α)
Step 2: Eliminate Implications (→)

Replace (α → β) with (¬α ∨ β)

α → β ≡ ¬α ∨ β
Step 3: Push Negation Inward

Apply De Morgan's Laws and Double Negation until ¬ only appears before literals:

De Morgan's Laws:
¬(α ∧ β) ≡ (¬α ∨ ¬β)
Negation of AND becomes OR of negations
¬(α ∨ β) ≡ (¬α ∧ ¬β)
Negation of OR becomes AND of negations
Double Negation:
¬¬α ≡ α
Step 4: Distribute ∨ over ∧

Apply the distributivity law to get conjunction of disjunctions:

Distributivity Law:
α ∨ (β ∧ γ) ≡ (α ∨ β) ∧ (α ∨ γ)
OR distributes over AND to produce CNF structure

Step-by-Step: Converting R₂

Convert: B₁,₁ ↔ (P₁,₂ ∨ P₂,₁)

Step 1: Eliminate ↔
(B₁,₁ → (P₁,₂ ∨ P₂,₁)) ∧ ((P₁,₂ ∨ P₂,₁) → B₁,₁)
Step 2: Eliminate →
(¬B₁,₁ ∨ P₁,₂ ∨ P₂,₁) ∧ (¬(P₁,₂ ∨ P₂,₁) ∨ B₁,₁)
Step 3: Push ¬ inward
(¬B₁,₁ ∨ P₁,₂ ∨ P₂,₁) ∧ ((¬P₁,₂ ∧ ¬P₂,₁) ∨ B₁,₁)

Applied De Morgan's: ¬(P₁,₂ ∨ P₂,₁) = (¬P₁,₂ ∧ ¬P₂,₁)

Step 4: Distribute ∨ over ∧
(¬B₁,₁ ∨ P₁,₂ ∨ P₂,₁) ∧ (¬P₁,₂ ∨ B₁,₁) ∧ (¬P₂,₁ ∨ B₁,₁)

Result: 3 clauses in CNF

Warning: Exponential Blowup! The conversion from an arbitrary sentence to CNF can produce a sentence that is exponentially larger than the original. The number of clauses can grow as 2ⁿ where n is the depth of nested equivalences.

Example 1: Simple Conversion

Convert: (A ∧ B) → C

Step 1: No biconditionals — skip
Step 2: Eliminate →
¬(A ∧ B) ∨ C
Step 3: Push ¬ inward (De Morgan's)
(¬A ∨ ¬B) ∨ C
Step 4: Already in CNF
(¬A ∨ ¬B ∨ C)

Result: Single clause (¬A ∨ ¬B ∨ C)

Example 2: Biconditional + Negation

Convert: ¬(P ↔ Q)

Step 1: Eliminate ↔
¬((P → Q) ∧ (Q → P))
Step 2: Eliminate →
¬((¬P ∨ Q) ∧ (¬Q ∨ P))
Step 3: Push ¬ inward (De Morgan's)
¬(¬P ∨ Q) ∨ ¬(¬Q ∨ P)
(P ∧ ¬Q) ∨ (Q ∧ ¬P)
Step 4: Distribute ∨ over ∧
(P ∨ Q) ∧ (P ∨ ¬P) ∧ (¬Q ∨ Q) ∧ (¬Q ∨ ¬P)
(P ∨ Q) ∧ (¬P ∨ ¬Q)

Result: Two clauses — either P or Q must be true, but not both

Example 3: Complex Formula

Convert: ((A → B) → C) ↔ D

Step 1: Eliminate ↔
(((A → B) → C) → D) ∧ (D → ((A → B) → C))
Step 2: Eliminate → (applied repeatedly)
(¬(¬(¬A ∨ B) ∨ C) ∨ D) ∧ (¬D ∨ (¬(¬A ∨ B) ∨ C))
Step 3: Push ¬ inward
((¬A ∨ B) ∧ ¬C ∨ D) ∧ (¬D ∨ ¬(¬A ∨ B) ∨ C)
((¬A ∨ B) ∧ ¬C ∨ D) ∧ (¬D ∨ (A ∧ ¬B) ∨ C)
Step 4: Distribute
(¬A ∨ B ∨ D) ∧ (¬C ∨ D) ∧ (¬D ∨ A ∨ C) ∧ (¬D ∨ ¬B ∨ C)

Result: 4 clauses

K-CNF Sentences

A K-CNF sentence is a CNF sentence where each clause has at most K literals.

K-CNF Definition:
Each clause Cᵢ has at most K literals
A restricted form of CNF useful for practical reasoning
TypeMax Literals per ClauseExample
1-CNF1(A) ∧ (¬B) ∧ (C)
2-CNF2(A ∨ B) ∧ (¬A ∨ C) ∧ (¬B)
3-CNF3(A ∨ B ∨ C) ∧ (¬A ∨ ¬B) ∧ (C)
Note: A K-CNF sentence where each clause has exactly K literals is a special case. The general definition says at most K.

Implicative Normal Form (Kowalski Form)

There is an alternative representation called Implicative Normal Form or Kowalski Form, where each clause is written as an implication.

Kowalski Form:
l₁ ∧ l₂ ∧ ... ∧ lₙ → head
Body (conjunction of negative literals) → Head (single positive literal)
Equivalent CNF:
¬l₁ ∨ ¬l₂ ∨ ... ∨ ¬lₙ ∨ head
Same clause, written as disjunction instead of implication
Kowalski ↔ CNF Equivalence
CNF: (¬A ∨ ¬B ∨ C)
Kowalski: A ∧ B → C

Both express the same clause. Kowalski form makes the if-then structure explicit.

CNF Form

• Written as disjunction of literals

• Better for resolution algorithm

• Uniform representation

Kowalski Form

• Written as if-then rule

• Better for human readability

• Foundation of logic programming
