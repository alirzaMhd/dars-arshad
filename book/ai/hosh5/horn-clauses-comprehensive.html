Horn Clauses

Artificial Intelligence — Logical Agents Chapter

What is a Horn Sentence?

A Horn sentence is a conjunction of Horn clauses. A Horn clause is a disjunction of literals in which at most one literal is positive.

Horn Clause Definition:
¬l₁ ∨ ¬l₂ ∨ ... ∨ ¬lₙ ∨ h
At most ONE positive literal (h). All others are negative (¬lᵢ)
Equivalent Implication Form:
l₁ ∧ l₂ ∧ ... ∧ lₙ → h
The conjunction of negative literals is the "body" → the positive literal is the "head"
Non-Horn Example: (A ∨ ¬B ∨ C) has TWO positive literals (A and C), so it is NOT a Horn clause.

Three Types of Horn Clauses

1. Definite Clause

Exactly ONE positive literal

¬l₁ ∨ ¬l₂ ∨ ... ∨ head

Equivalent to: l₁ ∧ l₂ ∧ ... → head

The "head" is the conclusion, the "body" is the premise

2. Fact (Unit Fact)

No negative literals at all

head (just the positive literal)

Equivalent to: True → head

An unconditional assertion known to be true

3. Integrity Constraint

NO positive literals

¬l₁ ∨ ¬l₂ ∨ ... ∨ ¬lₙ

Equivalent to: l₁ ∧ l₂ ∧ ... → False

Asserts something CANNOT be true simultaneously

Horn Clause Classification:
• Definite clause: exactly 1 positive literal
• Fact: 0 negative literals (just the head)
• Integrity constraint: 0 positive literals (all negative)

Horn Sentence = Conjunction of Horn Clauses

Horn Sentence Example
(¬A ∨ ¬B ∨ C) ∧ (¬D ∨ A) ∧ (B) ∧ (¬C)

This is a Horn sentence because each clause has at most one positive literal:

• Clause 1: (¬A ∨ ¬B ∨ C) — definite clause, head = C

• Clause 2: (¬D ∨ A) — definite clause, head = A

• Clause 3: (B) — fact

• Clause 4: (¬C) — integrity constraint (or goal clause)

Why Horn Clauses Matter

Reason 1: If-Then Rules

Every Horn clause can be written as an if-then rule (implication). This makes knowledge bases intuitive and readable.

¬A ∨ ¬B ∨ C ≡ A ∧ B → C

"If A and B are true, then C is true"

Reason 2: Logic Programming

Horn clauses are the foundation of logic programming (Prolog). Programs are written as definite clauses, and inference uses resolution.

Reason 3: Efficient Inference

Forward chaining and backward chaining both work in linear time O(n) for Horn clauses. This makes entailment decision cheap.

Reason 4: Closed Under Resolution

Resolving two Horn clauses always produces another Horn clause. This closure property is crucial for inference.

Horn Clauses as a Restricted CNF

Subset Relationship:
Horn Clauses ⊂ CNF ⊂ All Propositional Sentences
Every Horn clause is a CNF clause, but not every CNF clause is Horn

Linear-Time Inference

Key Result:
Deciding entailment with Horn clauses: O(n) time
Where n is the size of the knowledge base. This is dramatically faster than general propositional logic (which is NP-complete).

Forward Chaining

Data-driven: start from facts, derive new conclusions until query is found or no more inferences possible.

O(n) for Horn KB

Backward Chaining

Goal-driven: start from query, work backward to find supporting facts.

O(n) for Horn KB

Example 2: Wumpus World Horn Knowledge Base

Consider a simple Wumpus World scenario where the agent is at [1,1] and feels a breeze.

Given Facts:
B₁,₁ (breeze at [1,1])
R₁,₁ (agent is at [1,1])
Rules (Definite Clauses):
R₁,₁ ∧ (P₁,₂ ∨ P₂,₁) → B₁,₁ (breeze rule)

In CNF: (¬R₁,₁ ∨ ¬P₁,₂ ∨ B₁,₁) ∧ (¬R₁,₁ ∨ ¬P₂,₁ ∨ B₁,₁)

Query: Is P₁,₂ (pit at [1,2]) entailed?

The agent knows B₁,₁ and R₁,₁ are true. From the breeze rule, it can conclude that at least one of P₁,₂ or P₂,₁ must be true. But it CANNOT conclude P₁,₂ alone — the query is not entailed.

Important Limitations

Not All Sentences Are Horn! Many useful sentences cannot be expressed as Horn clauses. For example, "A or B" (without any implication structure) is not Horn because it has two positive literals.

What Horn CAN Express

• If-then rules with one conclusion

• Facts (unconditional truths)

• Integrity constraints (forbidden states)

What Horn CANNOT Express

• "A or B" (disjunction of positives)

• General equivalences (A ↔ B)

• Rules with multiple conclusions

Practical Takeaway:
Real-world KBs are often mostly Horn. When they are, use forward/backward chaining for O(n) inference. When not, use resolution or DPLL.
