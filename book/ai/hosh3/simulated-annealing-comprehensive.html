Simulated Annealing Algorithm

Simulated annealing combines the efficiency of hill climbing (quickly finding good regions) with the completeness of random walk (ability to escape local optima). It does this by allowing bad moves with a probability that decreases over time.

Pseudocode

function SIMULATED-ANNEALING(problem, schedule) returns a solution state current ← MAKE-NODE(INITIAL-STATE[problem]) for t = 1 to ∞ do T ← schedule(t) if T = 0 then return current next ← randomly selected successor of current ΔE ← VALUE[next] - VALUE[current] if ΔE > 0 then current ← next else current ← next only with probability e^(ΔE/T)

Key Decision Rules

Better Move (ΔE > 0)
Always accept the successor. This is the exploitation step — moving to a better state.
Worse Move (ΔE ≤ 0)
Accept with probability e^(ΔE/T). This is the exploration step — may escape local optima.
Acceptance Probability (for worse moves)
P(accept) = e^(ΔE / T)
ΔE = value[next] − value[current] (negative for worse moves). T = current temperature.

Understanding the Probability

  • When T is large (hot): e^(ΔE/T) ≈ e^(negative small number) ≈ 1 — almost all moves accepted, behaves like random walk
  • When T is moderate: moderately bad moves accepted, very bad moves rejected
  • When T is small (cool): e^(ΔE/T) ≈ e^(large negative) ≈ 0 — only good moves accepted, behaves like first-choice hill climbing
  • When T = 0: no bad moves accepted — identical to first-choice hill climbing

Temperature Schedule

The schedule function schedule(t) defines how temperature decreases over time. Common schedules:

ScheduleFormulaCharacteristic
Linear coolingT(t) = T₀ − αtSimple but may cool too fast
Exponential coolingT(t) = T₀ · α^tCommon, α in (0.8, 0.99)
Boltzmann coolingT(t) = T₀ / ln(t + 1)Theoretically guarantees convergence
The cooling schedule is critical. Cool too fast → early convergence to local optimum. Cool too slow → wasted computation. Finding the right schedule is part art, part science.

Physics Analogy: Annealing in Metallurgy

Simulated annealing is inspired by the physical process of annealing in metallurgy, where a material is heated and then slowly cooled to remove defects.

The Metallurgical Process

1. Heat
Metal is heated to a high temperature. Atoms become highly energetic and move freely, breaking existing bonds and exploring many configurations.
2. Controlled Cooling
Temperature is lowered gradually. Atoms lose energy and settle into stable positions. Slow cooling allows atoms to find low-energy crystal structures.
3. Low-Energy State
At room temperature, atoms are locked into a configuration with few defects — the lowest achievable energy state for the given cooling rate.

Why SA Works

At high temperatures, the algorithm explores broadly (like hot atoms moving freely), avoiding early convergence to poor local optima. As temperature decreases, it gradually shifts from exploration to exploitation, eventually settling into a good (often globally optimal) solution.

Convergence Property
lim_{t→∞} P(solution is global optimum) = 1
With a sufficiently slow cooling schedule (e.g., T(t) = T₀ / ln(t+1)), SA converges to the global optimum with probability approaching 1.
The theoretical guarantee requires infinitely slow cooling — impractical. In practice, we use faster schedules and accept that the optimality guarantee is approximate.

Example 2: VLSI Component Placement

Objective: Place 50 electronic components on a circuit board to minimize total wire length.

State: (x,y) coordinates for each component.

Cost function: Sum of Manhattan distances between connected components.

Three cooling schedules compared:

Fast cooling saves time but produces worse solutions. The medium schedule offers the best trade-off for this problem size.

Practical Guidelines for Cooling Schedule Design

ParameterGuidelineEffect
Initial THigh enough that ~80% of worse moves are acceptedEnsures sufficient initial exploration
Cooling rateα between 0.8 and 0.999 (exponential cooling)Slower = better solutions but more time
Iterations per TEnough to reach equilibrium at each temperatureMore = better exploration per level
Final TLow enough that no worse moves are acceptedEnsures convergence to a local optimum
There is no universal cooling schedule that works best for all problems. You must tune the schedule to your specific problem's landscape. Start with exponential cooling and adjust based on results.
