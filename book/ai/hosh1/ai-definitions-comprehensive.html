Four Definitions of AI

Artificial Intelligence — Chapter 1: AI and Intelligent Agents

The Four-Quadrant Classification of AI

The field of Artificial Intelligence can be organized along two key dimensions that produce four distinct definitions:

Thinking ← → Acting

Thinking Humanly

Modeling human cognitive processes — "the exciting new effort to make computers think like humans"

Thinking Rationally

Following logical laws of thought — "the study of the computations that make it possible to perceive, reason, and act"

Acting Humanly

Performing tasks indistinguishably from humans — "the art of creating machines that perform functions requiring human intelligence"

Acting Rationally

Achieving the best expected outcome — "computational intelligence is the study of the design of intelligent agents"

Human-like ← → Rational
The vertical axis distinguishes between thinking (internal reasoning processes) and acting (observable behavior). The horizontal axis distinguishes between human-like (matching human performance) and rational (achieving ideal outcomes regardless of human methods).

Warning: Common Mistakes

⚠ Mistake 1: Confusing "human-like" with "rational." Human thinking is often irrational (prone to biases). A rational system may think nothing like a human. For example, a chess engine evaluates millions of positions — no human does that.
⚠ Mistake 2: Assuming "acting humanly" (Turing Test) is the ultimate goal of AI. Many AI systems never need to mimic humans — an autopilot does not need to converse like a person.
⚠ Mistake 3: Thinking "thinking rationally" and "acting rationally" are the same. Thinking rationally is necessary but not sufficient for acting rationally — a rational agent must also perceive and act in the real world, not just reason about it.

Thinking Humanly: Cognitive Modeling

The "thinking humanly" approach asks: How does the human mind actually work, and can we replicate that in a computer?

The Cognitive Modeling Approach

  1. Introspection: Observe and describe one's own thought processes
  2. Psychological experiments: Measure human behavior under controlled conditions
  3. Brain imaging (fMRI, EEG): Observe neural activity during cognitive tasks
  4. Computer simulation: Build programs that produce the same inputs and outputs as human cognition
Key Insight:
Human cognitive process ≈ Computer program
If a program's step-by-step reasoning matches human protocols (think-aloud reports), it is a cognitive model of that human's thinking.

The General Problem Solver (GPS)

Developed by Allen Newell and Herbert Simon in 1961, GPS was one of the first AI programs. It aimed to be a universal problem-solving engine that could solve any well-defined problem through means-ends analysis.

Example 1: GPS Solving the Towers of Hanoi

The GPS program approaches this by comparing the current state to the goal state and identifying differences:

Step 1: Identify difference: Largest disk is on peg A, needs to be on peg C

Step 2: Select operator: Move disk from A to C

Step 3: Detect subgoal: Cannot move largest disk directly (smaller disks block it)

Step 4: Reduce difference: Clear peg C by moving smaller disks to B (recursive subproblem)

Step 5: Execute: Move n-1 disks A→B, move largest A→C, move n-1 disks B→C

What makes this "thinking humanly": Newell & Simon collected human think-aloud protocols and found that people solving the Towers of Hanoi use the same means-ends analysis strategy — comparing where they are to where they want to be and taking actions to reduce the gap.

Example 2: Comparing GPS Steps with Human Reasoning

Human Protocol (Think-Aloud)

"I need to get the big ring to the rightmost peg. But that little one is on top. So first I need to move the small one out of the way..."

GPS Trace

Goal: Move(Largest, A, C)
Subgoal: Clear(C)
Operator: Move(Small, A, B)
Proceed...

The matching structure of subgoal decomposition and difference reduction provides evidence that both follow the same cognitive algorithm.

Strengths of Thinking Humanly

+ Provides insights into human cognition

+ Validates psychological theories

+ Can explain how a result is reached

Weaknesses

− Hard to verify internal processes

− Human thinking is not always optimal

− Requires detailed behavioral data

⚠ Caution: A program that produces the correct answer does NOT necessarily think like a human. It may use completely different internal mechanisms. Deep Blue beat Kasparov at chess, but its brute-force search bears no resemblance to human intuition-based play. The "thinking humanly" approach requires matching the process, not just the outcome.

Acting Humanly: The Turing Test

Proposed by Alan Turing in his seminal 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," the Turing Test defines intelligence in terms of observable behavior: a machine is intelligent if a human interrogator cannot distinguish it from a human through conversation alone.

Turing Test Definition:
Machine passes ⇔ Human judge cannot reliably distinguish machine from human via text conversation
The machine is said to be intelligent if it can fool at least 30% of judges after 5 minutes of unrestricted conversation (Turing's original prediction for the year 2000).

Required Capabilities for Passing the Turing Test

Capability Purpose AI Subfield
Natural Language Processing (NLP) Understand and generate human language Natural Language Processing
Knowledge Representation Store information about the world Knowledge Representation & Reasoning
Automated Reasoning Draw conclusions and make inferences Automated Reasoning
Machine Learning Adapt to new situations and learn from experience Machine Learning
Computer Vision (for total test) Perceive and identify objects in the environment Computer Vision
Robotics (for total test) Manipulate objects and move in the physical world Robotics

The Full Turing Test vs. The Standard Turing Test

Turing originally proposed an "imitation game" using only text communication (teletype). However, some modern interpretations add a video signal to test visual perception (computer vision) and the ability to physically interact (robotics). This extended version is called the Full Turing Test.

Example 3: Analyzing a Turing Test Scenario

Scenario: A judge is communicating via text with two entities — Entity A and Entity B. One is a human, the other is an AI system called "CleverBot."

Judge: "What is 23 × 47?"

CleverBot: "Let me calculate... 23 times 47 is 1081." (This is correct, but how would a human likely respond?)

Human: "Uh, give me a second... about 1080 or so? Roughly 1100?" (Humans often approximate before giving exact answers.)

Judge: "Which one is the machine?"

Analysis: The judge might guess Entity A is the machine because it answered too perfectly too quickly. A human-like AI needs to account not just for correctness but also for human-like imperfections — hesitation, approximation, uncertainty.

Conclusion: Passing the Turing Test requires modeling human fallibility and conversational nuance, not just factual accuracy.

Example 4: Full Turing Test — A Robot Waiter Scenario

Scenario: A robot is designed to work as a waiter in a restaurant. Can it pass the Full Turing Test (with video signal and physical interaction)?

• NLP required: Take orders in natural language ("I'd like the steak medium-rare, please")

• Computer Vision required: Recognize which customer is speaking, see empty plates, navigate between tables

• Knowledge Representation: Remember table numbers, menu items, dietary restrictions

• Reasoning: Infer that a customer who asks for ketchup has food on their plate that might need it; resolve conflicts (two orders at once)

• Robotics: Carry trays without spilling, pour drinks, navigate crowded spaces

• Machine Learning: Learn customer preferences over time ("Mr. Smith always wants extra napkins")

Conclusion: The Full Turing Test demands integration of ALL major AI subfields working together seamlessly — a much harder challenge than text-only conversation.

⚠ Criticisms of the Turing Test:

1. Superficiality: Passing the test does not require genuine understanding — ELIZA (1966) fooled some users with simple pattern matching

2. Anti-human bias: Systems that are more rational than humans (e.g., perfect arithmetic) may be less likely to pass

3. Limited scope: Many intelligent systems (e.g., theorem provers, medical diagnosis) have no need to simulate human conversation

Key Requirements for Acting Humanly

• NLP — understand and produce language

• Knowledge representation — store world knowledge

• Automated reasoning — draw inferences

• Machine learning — adapt from experience

• Vision (full test) — perceive the environment

• Robotics (full test) — manipulate and move

Historical Turing Test Attempts

• ELIZA (1966): Pattern-matched therapist — fooled some users briefly

• PARRY (1972): Simulated paranoid schizophrenia — some psychiatrists fooled

• Eugene Goostman (2014): "Passed" at Royal Society — widely criticized

• ChatGPT (2022+): Most convincing to date, though not optimized for the test

Thinking Rationally: The Laws of Thought

The "thinking rationally" approach traces back to Aristotle (4th century BCE), who studied syllogisms — patterns of argument that always yield correct conclusions given true premises. This tradition led to formal logic as the foundation for rational thinking.

The Syllogism (Aristotle's Pattern):
All men are mortal. Socrates is a man. ∴ Socrates is mortal.
If both premises are true, the conclusion is guaranteed to be true by the form of the argument alone.

From Syllogisms to Formal Logic

Over centuries, logic evolved from Aristotle's syllogisms into a rich formal system:

Era Contributor Contribution
4th c. BCE Aristotle Syllogistic logic — first formal system for reasoning
17th c. Leibniz Dreamed of a "universal language" (characteristica universalis) for reasoning
19th c. Boole, Frege Propositional and predicate logic — mathematical foundations
20th c. Whitehead & Russell Principia Mathematica — all mathematics derivable from logic
1960s+ Robinson, McCarthy Resolution theorem proving, logic programming (Prolog)
Example 5: Aristotle's Syllogism — Formalized

In first-order predicate logic, the Socrates syllogism becomes:

Premise 1: ∀x (Man(x) → Mortal(x))

Premise 2: Man(Socrates)

Conclusion: Mortal(Socrates)

Derivation: From Premise 1, instantiate x = Socrates to get Man(Socrates) → Mortal(Socrates). From Premise 2, Man(Socrates) is true. By modus ponens, Mortal(Socrates) follows.

This is guaranteed correct — if the premises are true, the conclusion must be true, regardless of the meaning of "man" or "mortal."

Example 6: A More Complex Logical Derivation

Scenario: "If it rains, the ground gets wet. If the ground is wet, the grass grows. It is raining. Does the grass grow?"

Formalization:

P = "It rains"
Q = "Ground is wet"
R = "Grass grows"

Premise 1: P → Q
Premise 2: Q → R
Premise 3: P

By hypothetical syllogism (P → Q, Q → R ∴ P → R): P → R

By modus ponens (P, P → R ∴ R): R is true

Conclusion: Yes, the grass grows. This chain of reasoning is a logical proof — any rational agent thinking about the world should reach this conclusion.

Challenges: The Gap Between Logic and Real Reasoning

While formal logic provides a powerful framework for "thinking rationally," several major challenges limit its application:

Challenge 1: Informal Knowledge

Real-world knowledge is messy. Converting "You should not eat spoiled food" into formal logic is difficult — what is "spoiled"? How do you define exception cases? Much of human knowledge is tacit and hard to axiomatize.

Challenge 2: Theory vs. Practice

Even if knowledge can be formalized, logical reasoning is computationally expensive. First-order logic is semi-decidable — a theorem prover may run forever without reaching an answer. Practical systems must trade completeness for tractability.

Challenge 3: Uncertainty

Classical logic handles only absolute truth. But real-world reasoning requires handling uncertainty: "If I hear thunder, it is probably going to rain." Probability theory and fuzzy logic extend logic to handle degrees of belief.

Challenge 4: Commonsense Reasoning

Humans effortlessly use common sense: "If I drop my coffee cup, it will break." Encoding this as logical axioms requires an enormous (potentially infinite) set of statements about the world — a challenge known as the commonsense knowledge problem.

⚠ Common Mistake — Logic is NOT all of rationality:

Being rational means more than just being logical. A purely logical system might spend hours proving a theorem when a quick approximate answer would serve better in practice. Rationality requires balancing correctness (soundness) with resource constraints (time, memory). This is why "thinking rationally" (logic) and "acting rationally" (intelligent agents) are distinct definitions.

Acting Rationally: The Intelligent Agent Approach

The "acting rationally" definition is the most widely accepted in modern AI. It defines intelligence in terms of a rational agent: an entity that acts to achieve the best expected outcome based on what it knows and perceives.

Rational Agent Definition:
For each possible percept sequence, a rational agent selects the action expected to maximize its performance measure, given the evidence from the percept sequence and any built-in knowledge.
Percepts → Reasoning → Best Action — the agent takes in information and acts to achieve the best result.

Key Concepts

Concept Definition Example
Agent Anything that perceives its environment via sensors and acts via actuators A person (senses: eyes/ears; actuators: hands/legs), a robot (sensors: cameras; actuators: motors)
Percept A single input from the environment at a given moment Camera image, temperature reading, text message
Percept Sequence The complete history of all percepts the agent has received All frames from a surveillance camera over the past hour
Performance Measure The criterion that evaluates how well the agent is doing Points scored, safety rating, profit earned
Rational Action The action that maximizes expected performance Braking when a pedestrian steps into the road

Acting Rationally Subsumes Thinking Rationally

A crucial insight: acting rationally includes thinking rationally, but is broader. Reasoning is one tool for achieving rational action, but there are others:

Example 7: When Reflex Beats Reasoning

Scenario: A self-driving car detects a child running into the road. The car has 0.3 seconds to respond.

Pure reasoning approach (thinking rationally): Formalize the situation in logic, deduce all consequences of braking vs. swerving vs. accelerating, compute expected utility of each action. Time needed: too long. The car crashes before the reasoning completes.

Rational agent approach (acting rationally): The car uses a reflex — a pre-computed rule: IF obstacle directly ahead AND distance < threshold THEN brake immediately. This is not "thinking" in the deliberative sense, but it is the rational action given the time constraint.

Acting rationally requires thinking when time permits, but also includes reflexes, learned behaviors, and heuristics when speed is essential. Thinking serves action, not the other way around.

Example 9: Why Acting Rationally is the Preferred Definition

Consider four AI systems designed to play poker:

Thinking Humanly agent: Models human poker reasoning — hesitation patterns, tells, risk aversion based on psychological data

Acting Humanly agent: Mimics human play so opponents cannot tell it's an AI

Thinking Rationally agent: Computes Nash equilibrium strategies, game-theoretic optimal play

Acting Rationally agent: Uses whatever strategy maximizes expected winnings against actual opponents — may bluff, adapt, exploit weaknesses, and switch between rational and human-like play as needed

The rational agent subsumes the others: it can use cognitive modeling (thinking humanly) to predict opponents, it can mimic human behavior (acting humanly) when beneficial, and it can compute optimal strategies (thinking rationally) when time permits. Acting rationally is the most inclusive and practical definition.

⚠ Common Mistake — Rational vs. Perfect:

A rational agent is NOT the same as a perfect (omniscient) agent. A rational agent does the best it can with the information it has. If you buy a lottery ticket and lose, that does not mean the decision was irrational — the expected value was negative, but the outcome was uncertain. Rationality is about decision-making under uncertainty, not about always being right.
