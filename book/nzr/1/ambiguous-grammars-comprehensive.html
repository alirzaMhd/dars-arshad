Ambiguous Grammars

Ambiguity, Inherent Ambiguity, and Unambiguous Equivalents — Complete Guide

What is an Ambiguous Grammar?

A context-free grammar G is ambiguous if there exists at least one string w ∈ L(G) that has two or more distinct parse trees (or equivalently, two or more distinct leftmost derivations or rightmost derivations).

Formal Definition:
G is ambiguous iff ∃w ∈ L(G) with ≥ 2 different parse trees
Equivalently: ∃w with ≥ 2 different leftmost derivations, or ≥ 2 different rightmost derivations.
Key Point: Ambiguity is a property of GRAMMARS, not languages. A language can be described by both ambiguous and unambiguous grammars.
Necessary Condition for Ambiguity (Rule Form A → AA):
If a grammar has rules of the form A → AA and A is useful, then the grammar is necessarily ambiguous.
Necessary Condition for Ambiguity (Rule Forms A → AA and A → αA):
If a grammar has rules of the form A → AA and A → αA where α ∈ (V ∪ Σ) and variable A is useful, then the grammar is necessarily ambiguous.

Checking for Ambiguity

To show a grammar is ambiguous:

  1. Find a string w ∈ L(G)
  2. Show two different parse trees for w
  3. Or show two different leftmost derivations for w
Important:
There is NO algorithm to determine if an arbitrary CFG is ambiguous
The ambiguity problem for CFGs is undecidable in general.

Example 1: Classic Ambiguous Grammar

G: S → SS | aSb | bSa | λ

For w = abab, we can have two parse trees:

Parse Tree 1 (via S → SS):

S /|\ S S /| |\ a S b a | | λ S | λ

Parse Tree 2 (via S → aSb):

S /|\ a S b | S /|\ a S b | λ

G is AMBIGUOUS — two different parse trees for w = abab.

Example 2: Expression Grammar with Concrete Numbers

G: E → E+E | E*E | E/E | (E) | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5

For w = 5*3+2:

Parse Tree (a) — * has higher priority, evaluates to 17:

E /|\ E + E | | E * E /|\ | E * E 2 | | 5 3

Evaluation: (5*3) + 2 = 15 + 2 = 17

Parse Tree (b) — + has higher priority, evaluates to 25:

E /|\ E * E /|\ | E + E 2 | | 5 3

Evaluation: 5 * (3+2) = 5 * 5 = 25

G is AMBIGUOUS — two different parse trees for w = 5*3+2, giving different values (17 vs 25)!

Unambiguous Grammar for Expressions

Unambiguous Equivalent with Precedence

E → E+T | E-T | T

T → T*F | T/F | F

F → (E) | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5

This grammar enforces: * and / bind tighter than + and -, and all are left-associative.

Right-Associative Version

Right-Associative + and -

E → T+ET-E | T

This grammar makes + and - right-associative (if desired).

Example 3: Another Ambiguous Grammar

G: S → aS | Sa | d

For w = ada:

Parse Tree 1 (leftmost derivation):

S /|\ a S /|\ S a | d

Parse Tree 2 (rightmost derivation):

S /|\ S a /|\ a S | d

G is AMBIGUOUS.

Inherently Ambiguous Languages

A language L is inherently ambiguous if EVERY context-free grammar for L is ambiguous. No unambiguous CFG exists for such languages.

Classic Example: L = {aⁿbⁿcᵐ : n,m > 0} ∪ {aᵐbⁿcⁿ : n,m > 0}

This language is the union of two CFLs. Any CFG for L must generate strings like aⁿbⁿcⁿ which belong to BOTH parts of the union, causing ambiguity.

L is inherently ambiguous — no unambiguous CFG exists.

Properties of Inherently Ambiguous Languages

  • The family of unambiguous CFLs is NOT closed under union
  • The family of unambiguous CFLs is NOT closed under intersection
  • The family of unambiguous CFLs is closed under reversal (if L is unambiguous CFL, then Lᴿ is also unambiguous CFL)
  • Every regular language has an unambiguous grammar
  • Some linear languages are inherently ambiguous

Reversal of Grammars (Gᵣ)

Definition of Gᵣ:

If G is a CFG, then Gᵣ (obtained by reversing all RHS of productions) satisfies L(Gᵣ) = (L(G))ᴿ.

G: S→AB, A→aA|λ, B→bdB|λ

Gᵣ: S→BA, A→Aa|λ, B→Bdb|λ

Then L(G) = (L(Gᵣ))ᴿ — the languages are reversals of each other.

Example: L = {aⁿbᵐcᵏ : n=m or m=k}

This language is the union of {aⁿbⁿcᵏ} and {aⁿbᵐcᵐ}.

This language is inherently ambiguous.

Removing Ambiguity: Expression Grammar

Ambiguous: E → E+E | E*E | (E) | a

Unambiguous Equivalent (with precedence):

E → E+T | T

T → T*F | F

F → (E) | a

This grammar enforces: * binds tighter than +, and both are left-associative.

Removing Ambiguity: If-Then-Else

Ambiguous: S → if E then S | if E then S else S | other

The "dangling else" problem: for "if E₁ then if E₂ then S₁ else S₂", which "if" does the "else" match?

Unambiguous fix:

S → M | U

M → if E then M else M | other (matched else)

U → if E then S | if E then M else U (unmatched else)

Key Takeaways

Summary:
• Ambiguity = multiple parse trees for one string
• Inherently ambiguous = no unambiguous grammar exists
• Most practical grammars can be made unambiguous by restructuring
• Unambiguous grammars are preferred for parsing (deterministic algorithms)

Practice Test Problem 1: Inherently Ambiguous Language

Question: Which language is inherently ambiguous?

Languages T, V, C, A, Y are defined with specific recursive definitions over {a,b,c}:

T = {aⁿbⁿcᵐ : n,m ≥ 1}
V = {aᵐbⁿcⁿ : n,m ≥ 1}
C = {aⁿbᵐcᵐ : n,m ≥ 1}
A = T ∪ V
Y = {aⁿbⁿcⁿ : n ≥ 1}

Answer: T ∪ Y is inherently ambiguous.

Practice Test Problem 3: Ambiguous Expression Grammar

Question: Grammar <EXPR>→<EXPR>+<EXPR>|<EXPR>*<EXPR>|( <EXPR> )|a — Is it ambiguous?

This grammar has no enforced precedence between + and *, so the same expression can be parsed in multiple ways.

Answer: (2) it is ambiguous (can give different priorities to + and *)

Practice Test Problem 4: Regular Expressions Language

Question: The set of all regular expressions over {a,b} — what type of language is it?

Regular expressions can be described by a regular grammar, so the language of all valid regular expressions is itself regular.

Answer: (3) it is a regular language (can be generated by a regular grammar)
