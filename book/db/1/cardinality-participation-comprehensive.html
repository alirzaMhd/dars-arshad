Cardinality & Participation Constraints

Database Design — Relationship Constraints

1. Cardinality Ratios — Definition

Definition
The cardinality ratio specifies the maximum number of entity instances that can participate in a relationship. It defines the numerical mapping between entity sets.

Notation Styles

Function Notation
(1,1), (1,n), (m,n)
Used in many textbooks
Ratio Notation
1:1, 1:n, m:n
Common in ER diagrams

2. Four Cardinality Types

One-to-One (1:1)
1 : 1
Each entity instance on one side relates to at most ONE entity instance on the other side, and vice versa.

Example: Person ↔ Passport

  • Each person has at most one passport
  • Each passport belongs to at most one person
One-to-Many (1:n)
1 : n
One entity instance on the left can relate to MANY instances on the right, but each right instance relates to only ONE left instance.

Example: Department → Employees

  • One department has many employees
  • Each employee works in exactly one department
Many-to-One (n:1)
n : 1
Many entity instances on the left relate to ONE instance on the right. This is the inverse of 1:n.

Example: Employees → Department

  • Many employees belong to one department
  • Each department has many employees
Many-to-Many (m:n)
m : n
Entity instances on both sides can relate to multiple instances on the other side.

Example: Students ↔ Courses

  • Each student can take many courses
  • Each course can have many students

3. Determining Cardinality from Requirements

Process
Step 1: Identify the two entity sets involved
Step 2: Ask "How many X can relate to ONE Y?"
Step 3: Ask "How many Y can relate to ONE X?"
Step 4: Combine answers to determine ratio
Common Mistake
Do not confuse the direction. 1:n is NOT the same as n:1. The order matters!

1. Participation Constraints — Definition

Definition
A participation constraint specifies whether every entity instance in an entity set MUST participate in the relationship (total/mandatory) or MAY NOT participate (partial/optional).

2. Total vs Partial Participation

Total Participation (Mandatory)
ER Diagram: Double Line
Every entity instance MUST participate in the relationship
Also called "existence dependency"
Partial Participation (Optional)
ER Diagram: Single Line
Some entity instances MAY NOT participate in the relationship
Also called "optional participation"

3. Visual Representation

ER Diagram Notation
Total: Entity ═══ Relationship ═══ Entity (double line)
Partial: Entity ─── Relationship ─── Entity (single line)

4. Detailed Examples

Example 1: Total — Employee Works In Department
Employee ═══ WORKS_IN ═══ Department
Total participation on Employee side

Every employee MUST work in a department. No employee can exist without being assigned to a department.

Example 2: Partial — Employee Manages Department
Employee ─── MANAGES ═══ Department
Partial participation on Employee side

Only some employees manage departments. Most employees do NOT manage any department.

Example 3: Total — Student Enrolls In Course
Student ═══ ENROLLS_IN ═══ Course
Total participation on Student side

Every student MUST enroll in at least one course. A student cannot exist without taking any courses.

Example 4: Partial — Course Has Students
Course ─── HAS_STUDENTS ═══ Student
Partial participation on Course side

Some courses MAY NOT have any students enrolled. A new course might not have any students yet.

5. Key Points to Remember

Important Distinction
  • Total participation = "Every entity MUST participate" (mandatory)
  • Partial participation = "Some entities MAY NOT participate" (optional)
  • Total is about minimum constraint (min = 1), Partial is about minimum constraint (min = 0)

1. (Min, Max) Notation — Overview

Definition
The (min, max) notation specifies both minimum and maximum participation for each entity in a relationship. It combines cardinality and participation constraints.
min = 0
Partial participation (optional)
Entity MAY NOT participate
min = 1
Total participation (mandatory)
Entity MUST participate
max = 1
At most one relationship
Single-valued constraint
max = n
Many relationships allowed
Multi-valued constraint

2. All Four Combinations

Notation Participation Cardinality Meaning
(0, 1) Partial One Optional, at most one
(1, 1) Total One Mandatory, exactly one
(0, n) Partial Many Optional, many
(1, n) Total Many Mandatory, at least one

3. Detailed Examples

Example 1: Employee Works In Department
Employee(1,1) ─── WORKS_IN ─── Department(0,n)
Each employee works in exactly one department; a department can have zero or more employees.
  • Employee min=1: Every employee MUST work in a department (total)
  • Employee max=1: Each employee works in exactly ONE department
  • Department min=0: A department MAY have no employees (partial)
  • Department max=n: A department can have many employees
Example 2: Student Enrolls In Course
Student(1,n) ─── ENROLLS_IN ─── Course(1,m)
Each student takes at least one course; each course has at least one student.
  • Student min=1: Every student MUST take at least one course (total)
  • Student max=n: A student can take many courses
  • Course min=1: Every course MUST have at least one student (total)
  • Course max=m: A course can have many students
Example 3: Person Has Passport
Person(0,1) ─── HAS ─── Passport(1,1)
A person may have at most one passport; each passport belongs to exactly one person.
  • Person min=0: A person MAY NOT have a passport (partial)
  • Person max=1: A person has at most one passport
  • Passport min=1: Every passport MUST belong to a person (total)
  • Passport max=1: Each passport belongs to exactly one person
Example 4: Doctor Treats Patient
Doctor(0,n) ─── TREATS ─── Patient(0,n)
A doctor may treat zero or many patients; a patient may be treated by zero or many doctors.
  • Doctor min=0: A doctor MAY NOT treat any patient (partial)
  • Doctor max=n: A doctor can treat many patients
  • Patient min=0: A patient MAY NOT be treated by any doctor (partial)
  • Patient max=n: A patient can be treated by many doctors

4. Quick Reference

Remember
  • min tells you about participation (0=partial, 1=total)
  • max tells you about cardinality (1=one, n=many)
  • Always specify (min, max) for BOTH entities in a relationship

1. Worked Examples

Example 1: University — Student Takes Course
ER Diagram
Student(1,n) ─── TAKES ─── Course(1,m)
Attribute: Grade (on relationship)

Scenario:

  • Every student must take at least one course (total participation for Student)
  • A student can take many courses (max = n)
  • Every course must have at least one student (total participation for Course)
  • A course can have many students (max = m)
  • The Grade attribute is on the relationship because it depends on both student and course
Relational Schema
Student(StudentID, Name, ...)
Course(CourseID, Title, ...)
TAKES(StudentID, CourseID, Grade)
Example 2: Company — Employee Works In Department
ER Diagram
Employee(1,1) ─── WORKS_IN ─── Department(0,n)
Total participation for Employee, Partial for Department

Scenario:

  • Every employee must work in exactly one department (mandatory, single)
  • A department may have zero or many employees (optional, multiple)
  • A newly created department might not have any employees yet
Relational Schema
Employee(EmployeeID, Name, DeptID)
Department(DeptID, Name, ...)
Example 3: Hospital — Doctor Treats Patient
ER Diagram
Doctor(0,n) ─── TREATS ─── Patient(0,n)
Partial participation for both entities

Scenario:

  • A doctor may treat zero or many patients (optional, multiple)
  • A patient may be treated by zero or many doctors (optional, multiple)
  • A new doctor might not have any patients yet
  • A new patient might not be assigned a doctor yet
Relational Schema
Doctor(DoctorID, Name, ...)
Patient(PatientID, Name, ...)
TREATS(DoctorID, PatientID)
Example 4: Library — Member Borrows Book
ER Diagram
Member(0,1) ─── BORROWS ─── Book(0,1)
Partial participation for both, one-to-one mapping

Scenario:

  • A member may borrow at most one book (optional, single)
  • A book may be borrowed by at most one member (optional, single)
  • A member with no borrowed books: min=0
  • A book on the shelf: min=0
Relational Schema
Member(MemberID, Name, ...)
Book(BookID, Title, ...)
BORROWS(MemberID, BookID)
Example 5: School — Teacher Teaches Class
ER Diagram
Teacher(1,n) ─── TEACHES ─── Class(1,1)
Total participation for both entities

Scenario:

  • Every teacher must teach at least one class (total, multiple)
  • Every class must have exactly one teacher (total, single)
  • No class can exist without a teacher
  • No teacher can exist without teaching a class
Relational Schema
Teacher(TeacherID, Name, ...)
Class(ClassID, Room, TeacherID)

3. Converting (min, max) to Relational Schema

Rules for Conversion
1:1 → Foreign key in either table (prefer total side)
1:n → Foreign key in the "many" side table
m:n → Create a new junction/bridge table
Conversion Examples
-- 1:1: Person(0,1) --- HAS --- Passport(1,1)
-- Option: Put PassportID in Person table
Person(PersonID, Name, PassportID)

-- 1:n: Employee(1,1) --- WORKS_IN --- Department(0,n)
-- Put DeptID in Employee table
Employee(EmployeeID, Name, DeptID)

-- m:n: Student(1,n) --- TAKES --- Course(1,m)
-- Create junction table
TAKES(StudentID, CourseID, Grade)

4. Warning Boxes — Common Mistakes

Mistake 1: Confusing 1:n with n:1

1:n means "one on left, many on right"
n:1 means "many on left, one on right"
These are INVERSES of each other! The direction matters.

Mistake 2: Forgetting Both Entities Need (min, max)

You must specify constraints for BOTH sides of the relationship, not just one.

Mistake 3: Confusing Total with Mandatory Many

Total participation (min=1) ≠ max=n. Total means "must participate", not "must have many". A total, single constraint is (1,1).

Mistake 4: Ignoring min in Schema Design

The min value determines whether a foreign key can be NULL. If min=1 (total), the FK cannot be NULL.

5. Exam Tips

Key Strategies
✓ Always read requirements carefully for keywords like "every", "all", "must", "may"
✓ "Every/All/Must" → Total participation (min = 1)
✓ "Some/May/Might" → Partial participation (min = 0)
✓ "At most one" → max = 1
✓ "Can have many" → max = n
✓ Draw ER diagram first, then convert to schema
✓ For m:n, always create a junction table with FKs from both entities
Quick Checklist for Exam Questions
  • □ Identify both entity sets
  • □ Determine min for each entity (0 or 1)
  • □ Determine max for each entity (1 or n)
  • □ Write (min, max) for both sides
  • □ Draw ER diagram with correct lines
  • □ Convert to relational schema
  • □ Verify: Does the schema enforce the constraints?
