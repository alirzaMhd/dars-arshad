RELATIONSHIPS IN DATABASE DESIGN

Comprehensive Study Guide - Entity Relationship Model

Definition

Core Definition
Relationship = Association between two or more entities
A relationship connects entities and represents how they are logically related to each other in the real world.

Key Concepts

Relationship Type
Describes which entities participate and how

Defines the rules and constraints of the association. Example: ENROLLS_IN is a relationship type between Student and Course.

Relationship Set
Collection of all relationship instances

The set of all current associations of a given relationship type. Example: All current student-course enrollments in the university.

Relationship Instance
Association between specific entity instances

A single, specific link. Example: Student "Alice" enrolls in Course "DB101".

Degree of a Relationship

Degree = Number of entity types participating
Degree N → N entity types involved
Binary (Degree 2)

Two entity types. Most common in practice.

Student —— ENROLLS_IN —— Course
Ternary (Degree 3)

Three entity types participate simultaneously.

Doctor —— PRESCRIBES —— Patient
                               |
                               Medicine
N-ary (Degree N)

N entity types. Rarely used beyond ternary.

Entity1 —— Relationship —— EntityN

Relationship Attributes

Can relationships have their own attributes?
YES! Relationships can carry attributes.
A relationship can have descriptive attributes that provide additional information about the association itself.
Example: Student_Course Relationship

ENROLLS_IN connects Student to Course, and has attributes:

  • Enrollment_Date — When the student enrolled
  • Grade — The grade received
  • Semester — Which semester

These attributes belong to the relationship, not to Student or Course individually.

One-to-One (1:1)

Constraint
Each A relates to at most one B, AND each B relates to at most one A
Real-World Example: Person — HAS_PASSPORT — Passport

Each person has at most one passport. Each passport belongs to at most one person.

Another Example: Country — HAS_CAPITAL — City

Each country has one capital city. Each city is the capital of at most one country.

One-to-Many (1:n)

Constraint
Each A relates to many B, but each B relates to at most one A
Real-World Example: Department — EMPLOYS — Employee

One department employs many employees. Each employee belongs to at most one department.

Another Example: Author — WRITES — Book

One author can write many books. Each book has one author (simplified).

Many-to-One (n:1)

Constraint
Reverse of 1:n — Many A relate to one B
Real-World Example: Employee — WORKS_IN — Department

Many employees work in one department. Each department has many employees.

n:1 and 1:n are the same relationship viewed from different directions.

Many-to-Many (m:n)

Constraint
Each A relates to many B, AND each B relates to many A
Real-World Example: Student — ENROLLS_IN — Course

One student can enroll in many courses. One course can have many students enrolled.

Another Example: Doctor — TREATS — Patient

One doctor can treat many patients. One patient can be treated by many doctors.

Comparison Table

Type Notation Entity A Entity B Example
One-to-One 1:1 At most 1 B At most 1 A Person — Passport
One-to-Many 1:n Many B At most 1 A Department — Employees
Many-to-One n:1 At most 1 B Many A Employee — Department
Many-to-Many m:n Many B Many A Student — Course

Descriptive Attributes on Relationships

Key Concept
Relationships can have their own attributes that describe the association
These attributes do not belong to either entity — they belong to the relationship itself.
Example: Student — ENROLLS_IN — Course
ENROLLS_IN has attributes:
├── Grade (e.g., "A", "B+")
├── Semester (e.g., "Fall 2025")
└── Enrollment_Date (e.g., "2025-09-01")

Grade is NOT an attribute of Student (not all students have grades) and NOT an attribute of Course (not all courses have grades). It exists only when a student is enrolled in a course.

Key Attribute on Relationships

Can a relationship have a key attribute?
Yes, when the relationship itself needs unique identification
Example: Transaction_ID as Key on a Payment Relationship

Customer — MAKES_PAYMENT — Product

Each payment instance could have a Transaction_ID as a key attribute to uniquely identify each payment.

Composite Attributes on Relationships

Composite Attribute
An attribute that can be divided into sub-parts
Example: Employee — ASSIGNED_TO — Project
ASSIGNED_TO has attribute:
└── Assignment_Period
├── Start_Date
└── End_Date

Assignment_Period is a composite attribute composed of Start_Date and End_Date.

Multi-valued Attributes on Relationships

Multi-valued Attribute
An attribute that can hold multiple values for a single instance
Example: Doctor — TREATS — Patient
TREATS has attribute:
└── Treatment_Type (multi-valued)
├── "Chemotherapy"
├── "Radiation"
└── "Surgery"

A single doctor-patient relationship instance can have multiple treatment types.

Summary of Attribute Types on Relationships

Attribute Type Description Example
Simple Atomic, cannot be divided Grade = "A"
Composite Can be divided into sub-parts Assignment_Period → Start_Date + End_Date
Multi-valued Can hold multiple values Treatment_Type = {"Chemo", "Radiation"}
Key Uniquely identifies relationship instance Transaction_ID

Worked Example 1: University

Student — ENROLLS_IN — Course (m:n with Grade attribute)
ENTITIES:
Student (Student_ID, Name, Major)
Course (Course_ID, Title, Credits)

RELATIONSHIP:
ENROLLS_IN (m:n)
Attributes: Grade, Semester

ER NOTATION:
[Student] ——< ENROLLS_IN >—— [Course]
|
(Grade, Semester)

Why m:n? A student takes many courses, and a course has many students.

Worked Example 2: Company

Employee — WORKS_IN — Department (n:1)
ENTITIES:
Employee (Emp_ID, Name, Salary)
Department (Dept_ID, Dept_Name, Budget)

RELATIONSHIP:
WORKS_IN (n:1)
No additional attributes needed

ER NOTATION:
[Employee] ——< WORKS_IN >—— [Department]
(Many employees)            (One department)

Why n:1? Many employees work in one department. Each employee belongs to one department.

Worked Example 3: Hospital

Doctor — TREATS — Patient (m:n)
ENTITIES:
Doctor (Doc_ID, Name, Specialty)
Patient (Patient_ID, Name, DOB)

RELATIONSHIP:
TREATS (m:n)
Attributes: Treatment_Date, Diagnosis

ER NOTATION:
[Doctor] ——< TREATS >—— [Patient]
|
(Treatment_Date, Diagnosis)

Why m:n? A doctor treats many patients, and a patient can see many doctors.

Worked Example 4: Library

Member — BORROWS — Book (m:n with Date_Borrowed)
ENTITIES:
Member (Member_ID, Name, Phone)
Book (Book_ID, Title, Author)

RELATIONSHIP:
BORROWS (m:n)
Attributes: Date_Borrowed, Due_Date, Return_Date

ER NOTATION:
[Member] ——< BORROWS >—— [Book]
|
(Date_Borrowed, Due_Date, Return_Date)

Why m:n? A member borrows many books, and a book can be borrowed by many members (over time).

Worked Example 5: School

Student — ATTENDS — Class (1:n)
ENTITIES:
Student (Student_ID, Name, Grade_Level)
Class (Class_ID, Class_Name, Room)

RELATIONSHIP:
ATTENDS (1:n)
No additional attributes

ER NOTATION:
[Student] ——< ATTENDS >—— [Class]
(One student)            (Many students per class)

Why 1:n? Each student attends one class. Each class has many students.

ER Notation: Diamond Shape

Standard ER Diagram Notation
Relationships are represented as Diamond shapes
┌──────────┐                    ┌──────────┐
│  Student  │——————◇ ENROLLS_IN ◇——————│  Course   │
└──────────┘                    └──────────┘
     [Entity]       [Relationship]       [Entity]
  • Rectangle = Entity
  • Diamond = Relationship
  • Oval = Attribute
  • Line = Connection between entities and relationship

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1: Confusing 1:n with m:n

Do NOT assume every relationship is m:n. Analyze the real-world constraint carefully.

Example: An Employee works in ONE department → n:1, NOT m:n.

Mistake 2: Placing relationship attributes on entities

Grade belongs to the ENROLLS_IN relationship, NOT to Student or Course.

If you put Grade on Student, you lose the information about WHICH course the grade is for.

Mistake 3: Ignoring participation constraints

Always consider: Is participation total (mandatory) or partial (optional)?

A student MUST enroll in at least one course (total participation) vs. A student MAY enroll in a course (partial participation).

Mistake 4: Using ternary when binary suffices

Most real-world relationships are binary. Use ternary only when three entities must participate simultaneously.

Example: Doctor prescribes Medicine to Patient can often be modeled as two binary relationships.
