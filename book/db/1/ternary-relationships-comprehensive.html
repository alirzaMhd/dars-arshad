TERNARY RELATIONSHIPS

Advanced Database Design — Relationships of Degree 3

Definition

Core Concept
Ternary Relationship = Degree 3
A relationship that involves exactly three entity types simultaneously. All three entities participate together in a single association — removing any one breaks the meaning.
Critical Distinction
A ternary relationship is NOT equivalent to three separate binary relationships. Ternary captures a single, atomic association where all three entities are needed at once.

ER Notation

A ternary relationship is drawn as a diamond connected by lines to three entity rectangles. The diamond is labeled with the relationship name.

ER Diagram Sketch
┌──────────┐ │ SUPPLIER │ └────┬─────┘ │ │ SUPPLIES ┌──────┐ ├────────────< ◆ >├──────┤ │ │ PART │ │ └──┬───┘ │ │ │ │ └───────────────────┤ │ ┌────┴────┐ │ PROJECT │ └─────────┘

The diamond "SUPPLIES" connects to three entities. A single relationship instance links one supplier, one part, and one project together.

When Do We Need a Ternary Relationship?

A ternary relationship is needed when the combination of all three entities carries meaning that cannot be decomposed into pairwise relationships.

1. Doctor prescribes Medication to Patient
All three must be known:

Which doctor prescribed which medication to which patient? The prescription ties all three — not just pairs.

2. Supplier supplies Part to Project
The classic ternary example:

Supplier S1 provides Part P3 specifically for Project J5. The meaning requires all three at once.

3. Student takes Course in Semester
Time-bound enrollment:

Which student enrolled in which course during which semester? A student might take CS101 in Fall but not in Spring — the semester matters.

4. Agent sells Policy to Customer
Insurance industry:

An agent can sell different policies to different customers. The relationship requires all three parties simultaneously.

Formal Definition

Set Theory View
R ⊆ E₁ × E₂ × E₃
A ternary relationship R is a subset of the Cartesian product of three entity sets. Each relationship instance is a 3-tuple (e₁, e₂, e₃).
Example: Supplier-Part-Project
SupplierPartProjectQty
S1P1J1100
S1P2J2200
S2P1J1150
S3P3J250

Each row is a 3-tuple. You cannot express "S1 supplies P2 to J2" using only binary relationships without losing the connection between all three.

When to Use Ternary vs Multiple Binary Relationships

The key question: Does the association require all three entities at once, or are the pairs independent?

Use Ternary When
  • All three entities must be known to understand the relationship
  • The relationship has attributes that depend on all three
  • The combination of three is the atomic unit of meaning
  • Example: A prescription needs doctor + patient + medication
Use Multiple Binary When
  • Each pair has an independent, meaningful relationship
  • You can express the business rule with pairs alone
  • No attribute depends on all three simultaneously
  • Example: Student enrolls in Course, Course is taught by Professor — these are separate facts

Feature Ternary (Degree 3) Multiple Binary Relationships

Why Three Binary ≠ One Ternary

Common Exam Trap
Three binary relationships between three entities do NOT capture the same information as one ternary relationship. A ternary relationship records the simultaneous association of all three.
Demonstration: Loss of Information

Binary relationships (independent):

  • Supplier S1 → Part P1 (supplies)
  • Part P1 → Project J1 (used in)
  • Supplier S1 → Project J1 (supplies to)

This tells us three separate facts, but it does NOT tell us whether S1 supplies P1 specifically for J1, or for some other project.

Ternary relationship:

SUPPLIES(Supplier=S1, Part=P1, Project=J1)

This tells us the exact triple: S1 provides P1 to J1. The simultaneous association is explicit.

Formal Warning
Binary(S→P) ∩ Binary(P→J) ∩ Binary(S→J) ≠ Ternary(S, P, J)
The intersection of three binary relationships does not reconstruct the ternary relationship. Information about the triple is lost.

Cardinality Types

In ternary relationships, cardinality describes how many times an entity from one set can participate given fixed values from the other two sets.

(1,1,1)
All unique

Each combination of entities appears at most once. Every 3-tuple is unique across all three dimensions.

(1,1,n)
One entity repeated

For a fixed pair of two entities, the third can appear multiple times. E.g., one supplier-part pair can serve multiple projects.

(1,m,n)
Two entities repeated

Given one fixed entity, the other two can vary. E.g., one supplier can supply many parts to many projects.

(m,n,p)
Many-to-many-to-many

The most flexible: any entity can participate multiple times in combination with the others. The most common in practice.

Reading Cardinality in Ternary

Rule for Reading
Look at one entity set, fix the other two, and count
The cardinality on an entity's "side" describes how many relationship instances that entity can appear in for each combination of the OTHER two entities.
Example: Supplier(1), Part(1), Project(n)

If the cardinality is (1,1,n):

  • Supplier side = 1: For a given (Part, Project) pair, at most 1 supplier can supply that part to that project
  • Part side = 1: For a given (Supplier, Project) pair, at most 1 part can be supplied by that supplier to that project
  • Project side = n: For a given (Supplier, Part) pair, that supplier can supply that part to many projects
Cardinality Constraint Table
Fixed Entity Cardinality Interpretation
Supplier = S1, Part = P1 n (Project varies) S1 can supply P1 to multiple projects
Supplier = S1, Project = J1 1 (Part is fixed) S1 can supply only 1 part to J1
Part = P1, Project = J1 1 (Supplier is fixed) Only 1 supplier can supply P1 to J1

Participation Constraints

Each entity in a ternary relationship can have partial or total participation, just like in binary relationships.

Total Participation (double line)

Every entity instance must appear in at least one relationship instance.

Example: Every supplier must supply at least one part to some project.

Partial Participation (single line)

An entity instance may or may not appear in a relationship instance.

Example: Some projects might not have any supplier providing parts.

Common Mistake
In ternary relationships, participation constraints apply independently to each entity. Total participation of Supplier does NOT mean total participation of Part or Project.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Supplier-Part-Project (Classic Ternary)

Entities

  • Supplier (S_ID, S_Name, City)
  • Part (P_ID, P_Name, Color)
  • Project (J_ID, J_Name, Budget)

Relationship: SUPPLIES

Attributes: Qty (quantity supplied)

Cardinality: (1,1,n) — one supplier supplies one part to many projects

ER to Relational Conversion

SUPPLIES(S_ID FK → Supplier, P_ID FK → Part, J_ID FK → Project, Qty) PRIMARY KEY: (S_ID, P_ID, J_ID)

The primary key is the combination of all three foreign keys. This ensures each triple appears at most once.

Example 2: Doctor-Patient-Medication

Entities

  • Doctor (D_ID, D_Name, Specialty)
  • Patient (Pat_ID, Pat_Name, Age)
  • Medication (M_ID, M_Name, Dosage)

Relationship: PRESCRIBES

Attributes: Date, Instructions

Cardinality: (m,n,p) — many-to-many-to-many

Relational Schema

PRESCRIBES(D_ID FK → Doctor, Pat_ID FK → Patient, M_ID FK → Medication, Prescribe_Date, Instructions) PRIMARY KEY: (D_ID, Pat_ID, M_ID, Prescribe_Date)

A patient may receive the same medication from the same doctor on different dates, so date is part of the key.

Example 3: Student-Course-Semester

Entities

  • Student (St_ID, St_Name, Major)
  • Course (C_ID, C_Name, Credits)
  • Semester (Sem_ID, Year, Term)

Relationship: ENROLLS_IN

Attributes: Grade

Cardinality: (1,1,n) — each student takes each course at most once per semester

Relational Schema

ENROLLS_IN(St_ID FK → Student, C_ID FK → Course, Sem_ID FK → Semester, Grade) PRIMARY KEY: (St_ID, C_ID, Sem_ID)

A student can take the same course in different semesters, but not twice in the same semester. Grade depends on all three.

Example 4: Company-Product-Country (Market Relationship)

Entities

  • Company (Co_ID, Co_Name, HQ)
  • Product (Prod_ID, Prod_Name, Price)
  • Country (Ctry_ID, Ctry_Name, Continent)

Relationship: SELLS_IN

Attributes: Market_Share, Revenue

Cardinality: (1,m,n) — one company can sell many products in many countries

Relational Schema

SELLS_IN(Co_ID FK → Company, Prod_ID FK → Product, Ctry_ID FK → Country, Market_Share, Revenue) PRIMARY KEY: (Co_ID, Prod_ID, Ctry_ID)

Apple sells iPhone in USA with 55% market share, but in India with 8% market share. All three entities determine the attributes.

Example 5: Agent-Customer-Policy (Insurance)

Entities

  • Agent (A_ID, A_Name, Branch)
  • Customer (Cust_ID, Cust_Name, Phone)
  • Policy (Pol_ID, Pol_Type, Premium)

Relationship: SELLS

Attributes: Sale_Date, Commission

Cardinality: (m,n,p)

Relational Schema

SELLS(A_ID FK → Agent, Cust_ID FK → Customer, Pol_ID FK → Policy, Sale_Date, Commission) PRIMARY KEY: (A_ID, Cust_ID, Pol_ID, Sale_Date)

An agent can sell multiple policies to the same customer on different dates. The commission depends on agent, customer, and policy type combined.

Relational Conversion Rules

General Rule
Ternary R(E₁, E₂, E₃) → Table with FKs from all three + relationship attributes
Create a new relation containing: primary keys from all three entity sets (as foreign keys), plus any attributes on the relationship itself.
Primary Key Selection
PK = combination of entity PKs (or subset, depending on cardinality)
For (1,1,n): PK = (E₁_PK, E₂_PK). For (m,n,p): PK = all three. For (1,1,1): PK = all three.

Conversion Steps

  1. Create a new table for the ternary relationship
  2. Add the primary key of each participating entity as a foreign key column
  3. Add any attributes that belong to the relationship itself
  4. Determine the primary key based on cardinality constraints
  5. Add NOT NULL constraints for total participation entities
  6. Add unique constraints if cardinality requires it

Exam Tips & Common Mistakes

Mistake 1: Confusing Ternary with Three Binary
Students often decompose a ternary relationship into three binary relationships. This is wrong unless the ternary can be proven to be decomposable without loss.
Mistake 2: Wrong Primary Key
The PK of a ternary relationship table is NOT always all three foreign keys. Check cardinality first. For (1,1,n), only two FKs form the key.
Mistake 3: Misreading Cardinality Direction
Cardinality in ternary relationships is read by fixing the other two entities and counting how many times the third appears. Don't confuse it with binary cardinality.
Mistake 4: Ignoring Relationship Attributes
Attributes like Qty, Grade, Date, Commission belong to the relationship, not to any entity. They go on the ternary relationship table.
Mistake 5: Assuming All Ternaries Are Many-to-Many-to-Many
Always check the business rules. Many real-world ternary relationships have (1,1,n) or (1,m,n) constraints, not (m,n,p).

Quick Reference

Ternary = 3 entities at once

If you can't remove any entity and keep the meaning, it's ternary.

PK depends on cardinality

Always determine cardinality before choosing the primary key.

Relationship attributes go on the new table

Qty, Grade, Commission, etc. are not attributes of any entity.

Check decomposition possibility

Some ternaries CAN be decomposed; most cannot without information loss.
