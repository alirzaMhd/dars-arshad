Packet Scheduling

Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach — Kurose & Ross, Chapter 4

FIFO / FCFS (First-In-First-Out / First-Come-First-Served)

FIFO is the simplest packet scheduling discipline. Packets are transmitted in the exact order they arrive at the output link. A single queue is maintained; when the link becomes idle, the packet at the head of the queue is dequeued and transmitted.

In the FIFO queuing abstraction model, packets arriving at the link output queue wait for transmission if the link is currently busy transmitting another packet. When a packet is completely transmitted over the outgoing link (i.e., receives service), it is removed from the queue.

Operation:
Departure Order = Arrival Order
The first packet to arrive is the first to depart (no reordering).
Buffer Occupancy:
Q(t) = A(t) − D(t)
Q = current queue length, A = arrivals, D = departures up to time t
Common mistake: Assuming FIFO provides any quality-of-service differentiation. It does not — all packets are treated identically.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Basic FIFO Timing

Given Packets P1, P2, P3 arrive at times 0 ms, 2 ms, 3 ms. Packet transmission time is 4 ms each.

Step 1 P1 starts at t=0, finishes at t=4.

Step 2 P2 starts at t=4, finishes at t=8.

Step 3 P3 starts at t=8, finishes at t=12.

Result: Departure order: P1→P2→P3. Average delay = (4+6+9)/3 = 6.33 ms.

Example 2: Buffer Overflow

Given A router has a FIFO buffer capacity of 3 packets. Arrivals: 5 packets arrive simultaneously at t=0. Output link rate = 1 packet/ms.

Step 1 3 packets enter the buffer; 2 packets are dropped.

Step 2 At t=1, first packet departs, a dropped spot opens — but the 2 dropped packets are already gone.

Result: 2 packets dropped (tail drop). Drop policy is critical in FIFO.

Example 3: Bursty Traffic Effect

Given Link rate = 1 Mbps. Four 1000-bit packets arrive back-to-back at t=0. Two more 1000-bit packets arrive at t=3 ms.

Step 1 First four packets: transmission takes 1 ms each. P1 finishes at 1ms, P2 at 2ms, P3 at 3ms, P4 at 4ms.

Step 2 P5 and P6 (arriving at t=3ms) wait until P4 finishes at 4ms. P5 from 4-5ms, P6 from 5-6ms.

Result: P5 delay = 2ms, P6 delay = 3ms. Bursty arrivals cause non-uniform delays even under FIFO.

Key Facts

• No traffic differentiation

• Tail drop on overflow

• O(1) enqueue/dequeue

• Single queue shared by all

Pros & Cons

+ Simple, fast, fair in order

- No QoS, no isolation

- Bursty flows hurt everyone

Priority Queuing (PQ)

Packets are classified into priority classes (e.g., high, low). The scheduler always transmits packets from the highest non-empty priority class first. This is non-preemptive — once a packet begins transmission, it finishes even if a higher-priority packet arrives.

In practice, a network operator may configure a queue so that packets carrying network management information (as indicated by the source or destination TCP/UDP port number) receive priority over user traffic; additionally, real-time voice-over-IP packets might receive priority over non-real-time traffic such as e-mail packets.

Service Rule:
Always serve highest priority class with non-empty queue
Lower classes get service only when all higher classes are empty.
Delay for Priority Class k:
E[Dk] = (Σi=1..k ρi) × RTT / (1 − Σi=1..k-1 ρi)
Approximate mean delay; ρ = utilization per class.
Common mistake: Forgetting PQ is non-preemptive. A low-priority packet currently being transmitted will NOT be interrupted by a newly arrived high-priority packet.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Two-Class Priority

Given High priority: 2 packets (P1,P2). Low priority: 3 packets (P3,P4,P5). All arrive at t=0. Transmission time = 2 ms per packet.

Step 1 High class served first: P1 at 0-2ms, P2 at 2-4ms.

Step 2 Low class served next: P3 at 4-6ms, P4 at 6-8ms, P5 at 8-10ms.

Result: High priority avg delay = 2ms. Low priority avg delay = 7ms. Significant disparity.

Example 2: Non-Preemptive Behavior

Given Low-priority P1 starts transmission at t=0 (2ms duration). High-priority P2 arrives at t=0.5ms.

Step 1 P1 is already being transmitted — it is NOT interrupted.

Step 2 P1 finishes at t=2ms. P2 then starts at t=2ms.

Result: P2 waits 1.5ms despite being highest priority. Starvation is avoided for the low class only at the packet level.

Example 3: Three Priority Classes

Given Classes: High (H), Medium (M), Low (L). At t=0: H has 1 pkt, M has 2 pkts, L has 4 pkts. Each pkt = 1ms transmission.

Step 1 H served: 0-1ms.

Step 2 M served: 1-3ms (both M packets).

Step 3 L served: 3-7ms (all 4 L packets).

Result: H delay=0.5ms avg, M delay=1.5ms avg, L delay=5ms avg. Low class can experience starvation if higher classes are persistently busy.

Key Facts

• Non-preemptive

• Strict priority service

• Low classes can starve

• Used in DiffServ (EF, AF)

Pros & Cons

+ Low delay for critical traffic

- Starvation for low classes

- Complex classification needed

Round Robin (RR) Scheduling

Round Robin serves packets from multiple classes in cyclic order. If there are N classes, the scheduler visits class 1, then 2, ... then N, then back to 1. At each visit, it transmits one packet from that class (if one is waiting). RR is work-conserving — if a class has no packet, the scheduler moves to the next class immediately.

A work-conserving queuing discipline will never allow the link to remain idle whenever there are packets (of any class) queued for transmission. A work-conserving round robin discipline that looks for a packet of a given class but finds none will immediately check the next class in the round robin sequence.

Service Cycle:
Cycle = [Class 1 → Class 2 → ... → Class N] repeated
Each active class gets exactly one packet per round (assuming all classes have packets).
Work-Conserving Property:
If queue i is empty, skip to queue i+1 immediately
The link is never idle if any queue is non-empty.
Common mistake: Confusing Round Robin with weighted fair queuing. In pure RR, all classes get equal turns regardless of weight or priority.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Three Active Queues

Given Queues A, B, C each have multiple packets. Packet sizes: all 1 KB. Link rate = 1 KB/ms.

Step 1 Round 1: A(0-1ms), B(1-2ms), C(2-3ms).

Step 2 Round 2: A(3-4ms), B(4-5ms), C(5-6ms).

Result: Each class gets 1/3 of link bandwidth equally. Fair distribution.

Example 2: Unequal Packet Sizes

Given Queue A: 1500-byte packets. Queue B: 500-byte packets. Link rate = 1000 bytes/ms.

Step 1 A transmits 1500 bytes (1.5ms), B transmits 500 bytes (0.5ms).

Step 2 Over 10 rounds: A sends 15000 bytes, B sends 5000 bytes.

Result: A gets 3x the bandwidth of B! RR gives equal turns, NOT equal bandwidth when packet sizes differ. This is a key drawback.

Example 3: Work-Conserving Behavior

Given Queues A (4 pkts), B (0 pkts), C (2 pkts). Packet size = 1ms each.

Step 1 A(0-1ms), B has none → skip, C(1-2ms).

Step 2 Back to A(2-3ms), C(3-4ms), A(4-5ms), C(5-6ms), A(6-7ms).

Result: When B is empty, A and C get more frequent service. The link is always busy — work-conserving.

Key Facts

• Equal turns per class

• Work-conserving

• No starvation

• Sensitive to packet size

Pros & Cons

+ Fairness (per-packet)

+ No starvation

- Unfair with variable sizes

Weighted Fair Queuing (WFQ)

WFQ is a generalization of Round Robin where each class (flow) is assigned a weight wi. The scheduler services flows in proportion to their weights, achieving both fairness and bandwidth guarantees. WFQ approximates the ideal "Generalized Processor Sharing" (GPS) model using packet-by-packet emulation [Demers 1990; Parekh 1993].

The description of WFQ has been idealized, as we have not considered the fact that packets are discrete and a packet's transmission will not be interrupted to begin transmission of another packet.

Bandwidth Fraction:
Fractioni = wi / Σj wj
Class i receives this fraction of the link bandwidth when all classes are backlogged.
Minimum Guaranteed Throughput:
Ri = R × wi / Σj wj
R = output link rate. Class i is guaranteed at least this rate when it has packets to send.
Virtual Finish Time (for packet-by-packet WFQ):
Fik = max(Fik-1, v(t)) + Lik / wi
F = finish number, v(t) = virtual time, L = packet length, w = weight. Packets are transmitted in increasing order of finish numbers.
Common mistake: Thinking WFQ guarantees an absolute bandwidth regardless of other flows. The guarantee is relative — if no other flows are active, a flow can get more than its weight proportion.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Two Flows with Different Weights

Given Link rate R = 10 Mbps. Two flows: w1 = 3, w2 = 1. Both are continuously backlogged.

Step 1 Σwj = 3 + 1 = 4.

Step 2 Flow 1 fraction = 3/4 = 0.75. Flow 2 fraction = 1/4 = 0.25.

Step 3 Flow 1 throughput = 10 × 0.75 = 7.5 Mbps. Flow 2 throughput = 10 × 0.25 = 2.5 Mbps.

Result: Flow 1 gets 3x the bandwidth of Flow 2, matching the weight ratio 3:1.

Example 2: Minimum Guarantee in Action

Given Link rate R = 100 Mbps. Three flows with weights: w = [5, 3, 2].

Step 1 Σw = 5+3+2 = 10.

Step 2 Minimum guarantees: R1 = 100×5/10 = 50 Mbps, R2 = 100×3/10 = 30 Mbps, R3 = 100×2/10 = 20 Mbps.

Step 3 If only Flow 1 is active, it gets the full 100 Mbps (better than minimum).

Result: Minimum guarantees hold when flows contend. Unused bandwidth is redistributed.

Example 3: Packet-by-Packet WFQ Ordering

Given Two flows: Flow A (w=2), Flow B (w=1). Link rate = 1 pkt/ms. At t=0: A has pkt of 1000 bytes, B has pkt of 500 bytes. Virtual time v(t) starts at 0.

Step 1 Compute finish numbers: FA = 0 + 1000/2 = 500. FB = 0 + 500/1 = 500.

Step 2 Equal finish numbers — tie-break arbitrarily. Suppose A goes first (0-1ms).

Step 3 At t=1ms, B transmits (1-1.5ms).

Result: A sent 1000 bytes, B sent 500 bytes in the first 1.5ms — ratio 2:1 matching weights.

Key Facts

• Weighted bandwidth shares

• Minimum rate guarantees

• GPS approximation

• Used in carrier networks

Pros & Cons

+ Strong fairness

+ QoS guarantees

- Higher complexity

- Per-flow state needed

Net Neutrality & Packet Scheduling

Net neutrality is the principle that Internet Service Providers (ISPs) should treat all data on the Internet equally, without discriminating or charging differently by user, content, website, platform, or application. Packet scheduling disciplines are the technical mechanisms that implement — or violate — this principle.

FCC 2015 Bright-Line Rules (Open Internet Order):
No Blocking · No Throttling · No Paid Prioritization
(1) No Blocking: a person engaged in the provision of broadband Internet access service shall not block lawful content, applications, services, or non-harmful devices. (2) No Throttling: shall not impair or degrade lawful Internet traffic on the basis of Internet content, application, or service, or use of a non-harmful device. (3) No Paid Prioritization: shall not engage in paid prioritization — the management of a broadband consumer's Internet traffic to favor particular content, applications, services, or devices [Smithsonian 2017].
FCC 2017 Restoring Internet Freedom Order:
Repealed Title II classification · Eliminated bright-line rules · Adopted transparency-only regime
The 2017 order reclassified broadband Internet access service as an information service (rather than a telecommunications service under Title II), eliminated the bright-line rules against blocking, throttling, and paid prioritization, and replaced them with a transparency requirement [Faulhaber 2012; Economides 2017; Madhyastha 2017].

Real-World Examples of Violations

Example 1: Comcast vs. BitTorrent (2007)

Comcast was found to be blocking or throttling BitTorrent peer-to-peer traffic using deep packet inspection. Packets were identified as BitTorrent and either delayed or dropped by the ISP's scheduling policy. This was a clear violation of the no-blocking principle (later codified in 2015).

Outcome: FCC issued a cease-and-desist. This case triggered the net neutrality movement.

Example 2: Vonage vs. ISPs (2004-2006)

Several ISPs blocked or degraded VoIP traffic from Vonage to protect their own voice services. The ISPs used priority queuing internally for their VoIP while dropping or deprioritizing Vonage packets — a textbook case of paid prioritization and anti-competitive throttling.

Outcome: FCC ordered ISPs to stop blocking Vonage under the "four freedoms" policy.

Example 3: The "Fast Lane" Debate

A hypothetical ISP implements WFQ with weights: Premium content w=10, Standard content w=1. Netflix pays for premium; a startup streaming service does not. Netflix gets R × 10/11 of the bandwidth while the startup gets R × 1/11 — even if users explicitly want the startup.

Outcome: This is exactly what the 2015 no-paid-prioritization rule prohibited. Under the 2017 order, ISPs could legally offer such arrangements.

Key Terms

• Blocking: ISP prevents access to content

• Throttling: ISP intentionally slows traffic

• Paid Prioritization: ISP charges for faster delivery

• Title II: Common carrier regulation

Connection to Scheduling

• FIFO = most neutral (no discrimination)

• PQ = potential for paid prioritization

• WFQ = can implement fast/slow lanes

Scheduling is the enforcement mechanism

Net neutrality is not about scheduling algorithms themselves — it is about how they are configured. A WFQ scheduler is neutral if all flows get weight = 1. It becomes a neutrality issue when weights are sold for profit.

Common Mistakes — Warning Box

FIFO = No QoS. Don't use FIFO when you need service differentiation.
Priority Queuing = Starvation risk. Low-priority traffic may never be served under persistent high-priority load.
Round Robin ≠ equal bandwidth. Equal turns with unequal packet sizes gives unequal bandwidth.
WFQ guarantees are relative. If Σw changes (flows appear/disappear), the actual rate changes.
Net neutrality ≠ technology. Scheduling is neutral; it's the policy that determines neutrality.
