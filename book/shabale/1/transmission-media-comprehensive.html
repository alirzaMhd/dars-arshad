Transmission Media

Wireless • Wired • Complete Guide for Exam Preparation

What is Transmission Media?

Definition:
Transmission media are the physical pathways through which data signals travel from a sender to a receiver in a network.
Also called communication channels or transmission channels. They are the "road" on which data bits travel between devices.

Two Main Categories

WIRELESS

• Uses electromagnetic waves (radio, microwave, infrared)

• No physical cable needed

• Suitable for mobile devices

• Prone to interference and eavesdropping

WIRED

• Uses physical cables (copper, fiber, coaxial)

• Requires physical connection

• Higher security and bandwidth

• More expensive to install

Key Exam Point: Transmission media are at Layer 1 (Physical Layer) of the OSI model. They carry raw bit streams, not formatted data.

Criteria for Choosing Transmission Media

Criterion What It Means
Bandwidth Maximum data rate the medium can support (measured in bps)
Transmission Distance Maximum distance before signal degradation requires amplification
Interference (EMI/RFI) Susceptibility to electromagnetic or radio-frequency interference
Security How easily can an unauthorized party intercept the signal?
Cost Cost of cable, connectors, installation, and maintenance
Ease of Installation How complex is the deployment process?

WIRELESS Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11)

Definition:
Wi-Fi is a wireless networking technology that uses radio waves (2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands) to provide high-speed wireless Internet and network connections.
Based on IEEE 802.11 standards. Operates in the ISM (Industrial, Scientific, Medical) radio bands.

Characteristics

Advantages

• Mobility — connect anywhere in range

• Easy to install (no cables)

• Scalable — add devices easily

• Supports roaming

Disadvantages

• Limited bandwidth vs wired

• Security vulnerabilities

• Interference from walls/objects

• Limited range

Wi-Fi Range Formula:
Range = Base_Distance × (λ / d)²
λ = wavelength, d = obstacle size. Higher frequency (5 GHz) has shorter range but higher throughput.

WIRELESS Bluetooth

Definition:
Bluetooth is a short-range wireless technology standard for exchanging data over short distances using UHF radio waves in the ISM band (2.402–2.480 GHz).
Defined by IEEE 802.15.1. Uses frequency-hopping spread spectrum (FHSS) to reduce interference.

• Very limited range (10-300m)

• Low data rate

WIRELESS Microwave Communication

Definition:
Microwave transmission uses high-frequency radio waves (1 GHz – 300 GHz) that travel in straight lines (line-of-sight) between two directional antennas.
Used for point-to-point communication. Requires clear line-of-sight between transmitter and receiver.

Two Types of Microwave

Terrestrial (Ground-based)

• Tower-to-tower transmission

• Towers placed 30-50 km apart

• Affected by weather (rain fade)

• Frequency: 2-40 GHz

Satellite

• Signal sent to satellite, relayed back

• Covers vast areas

• Higher latency (500+ ms round trip)

• Used for TV, GPS, weather

Characteristics

Advantages

• High bandwidth (up to 1+ Gbps)

• Faster to deploy than cables

• Good for remote areas

• Low error rate

Disadvantages

• Requires line-of-sight

• Affected by weather (rain, fog)

• Tower maintenance costs

• Limited capacity per link

Line-of-Sight Distance:
d = √(2 × R × h)
R = Earth's radius (6371 km), h = antenna height (km). For a 50m tower: d = √(2 × 6371 × 0.05) ≈ 25.2 km
Frequency Band for Microwave:
1 GHz to 300 GHz (wavelength: 1mm to 30cm)
Most common bands: C-band (4-8 GHz), Ku-band (12-18 GHz), Ka-band (26-40 GHz)

WIRELESS Satellite Communication

Definition:
Satellite communication uses orbiting satellites to relay signals between ground stations (uplink and downlink).
Signals are amplified and retransmitted by the satellite. Enables global coverage including oceans and remote areas.

Satellite Orbits

Orbit Altitude Latency Coverage Example Use
LEO 160-2000 km ~20-50 ms Small area (constellation needed) Starlink, Iridium
MEO 2000-35786 km ~100-200 ms Regional GPS, Galileo
GEO 35786 km ~240-280 ms One-third of Earth DirecTV, Inmarsat

Characteristics

Advantages

• Global coverage

• Reach remote/unreachable areas

• Broadcast capability

• Independent of ground infrastructure

Disadvantages

• High latency (especially GEO)

• Expensive to launch and maintain

• Weather affects signal

• Limited bandwidth per satellite

Satellite Link Budget:
P_rx = P_tx + G_tx - L_path + G_rx
P_rx = received power, P_tx = transmit power, G = antenna gains, L_path = path loss
Geostationary Orbit Condition:
Orbital period = 24 hours (matches Earth's rotation)
At 35,786 km altitude, the satellite appears stationary relative to Earth's surface.

WIRED Twisted Pair Cable

Definition:
A twisted pair consists of two insulated copper wires twisted together to reduce electromagnetic interference and crosstalk.
The most common networking cable. Used in Ethernet (RJ-45 connectors) and telephone systems (RJ-11).

Two Types

UTP (Unshielded Twisted Pair)

• No additional shielding

• Most common (Cat5e, Cat6)

• Flexible, easy to install

• Cheaper

STP (Shielded Twisted Pair)

• Foil/braid shielding around pairs

• Better EMI protection

• Thicker, less flexible

• More expensive

• Security risk (can be tapped)

Maximum Cable Length (Ethernet):
L_max = 100 meters
Standard limit for copper Ethernet. Beyond this, signal attenuation causes errors. Use repeaters/switches for longer distances.

WIRED Coaxial Cable

Definition:
A coaxial cable has a central copper conductor surrounded by insulation, a metallic shield, and an outer jacket.
The shield provides excellent EMI protection. Used for cable TV, broadband Internet, and older Ethernet (10BASE2/10BASE5).

WIRED Fiber Optic Cable

Definition:
Fiber optic cables transmit data as pulses of light through thin strands of glass or plastic using total internal reflection.
The highest-bandwidth transmission medium. Immune to EMI. Used for long-distance, high-speed networking.

Two Types

Single-Mode Fiber (SMF)

• Core diameter: 8-10 μm

• Single light path (mode)

• Longer distances (up to 100+ km)

• Laser light source

• More expensive

Multi-Mode Fiber (MMF)

• Core diameter: 50-62.5 μm

• Multiple light paths (modes)

• Shorter distances (up to 2 km)

• LED light source

• Less expensive

Fiber Optic Comparison

Feature Single-Mode Multi-Mode
Core Size 8-10 μm 50-62.5 μm
Light Source Laser LED
Distance Up to 100+ km Up to 2 km
Bandwidth 100+ Gbps 10-100 Gbps
Cost Higher Lower
Use Case Long-haul, telecom LAN, data centers

Characteristics

Advantages

• Extremely high bandwidth (100+ Gbps)

• Very low attenuation (0.2 dB/km)

• Immune to EMI/RFI

• High security (hard to tap)

• Long distances (100+ km)

Disadvantages

• Expensive cable and equipment

• Fragile (glass core)

• Specialized tools needed

• Difficult to splice

Fiber Optic Attenuation:
Loss(dB) = α × L
α = attenuation coefficient (dB/km), L = length (km). Single-mode: ~0.2 dB/km, Multi-mode: ~3 dB/km
Total Internal Reflection:
θ > θc = arcsin(n₂/n₁)
Light must strike the core-cladding boundary at an angle greater than the critical angle (θc) to stay trapped in the core. n₁ = core refractive index, n₂ = cladding refractive index.

Key Formulas for Transmission Media

1. Shannon's Channel Capacity:
C = B × log₂(1 + SNR)
C = channel capacity (bps), B = bandwidth (Hz), SNR = signal-to-noise ratio. Maximum theoretical data rate for a noisy channel.
2. Nyquist Bit Rate (noiseless channel):
Bit Rate = 2 × B × log₂(L)
B = bandwidth (Hz), L = number of signal levels. Maximum bit rate without inter-symbol interference.
3. Free Space Path Loss (FSPL):
FSPL(dB) = 20×log₁₀(d) + 20×log₁₀(f) + 20×log₁₀(4π/c)
d = distance (m), f = frequency (Hz), c = speed of light (3×10⁸ m/s). For f in MHz and d in km: FSPL(dB) = 20×log₁₀(d) + 20×log₁₀(f) + 32.45
4. Fiber Optic Attenuation:
Loss(dB) = α × L
α = attenuation coefficient (dB/km), L = cable length (km). SMF: ~0.2 dB/km, MMF: ~3 dB/km
5. Link Budget:
Received Power = Tx Power + Tx Gain - Path Loss + Rx Gain
All values in dBm or dB. Determines if a wireless link is feasible.
6. Cable Length Limit (Twisted Pair):
L_max = 100 meters (Ethernet standard)
Beyond 100m, signal attenuation causes bit errors. Use switches/repeaters to extend.
7. Satellite Propagation Delay:
Delay = 2 × d / c
d = distance to satellite (m), c = speed of light. GEO (35786 km): ~240 ms one-way.
8. Wavelength:
λ = c / f
λ = wavelength (m), c = speed of light (3×10⁸ m/s), f = frequency (Hz). Higher frequency = shorter wavelength.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Shannon's Capacity Calculation

A wireless channel has a bandwidth of 20 MHz and an SNR of 30 dB. What is the maximum data rate?

Given: B = 20 MHz = 20×10⁶ Hz, SNR = 30 dB

Step 1: Convert SNR from dB to linear: SNR_linear = 10^(30/10) = 1000

Step 2: Apply Shannon's formula: C = 20×10⁶ × log₂(1 + 1000)

C = 20×10⁶ × log₂(1001) ≈ 20×10⁶ × 9.97

C ≈ 199.4 Mbps

This is the theoretical maximum. Real throughput is lower due to protocol overhead.

Example 2: Nyquist Bit Rate

A noiseless twisted pair cable has a bandwidth of 100 MHz. If 4 voltage levels are used, what is the maximum bit rate?

Given: B = 100 MHz = 100×10⁶ Hz, L = 4 levels

Step 1: Calculate bits per symbol: log₂(4) = 2 bits/symbol

Step 2: Apply Nyquist formula: Bit Rate = 2 × 100×10⁶ × 2

Bit Rate = 400 Mbps

With 8 levels: log₂(8) = 3 bits/symbol → Bit Rate = 600 Mbps

Example 3: Fiber Optic Link Loss Budget

A single-mode fiber link spans 40 km. The transmitter outputs +3 dBm, and the receiver sensitivity is -28 dBm. Connector loss is 0.5 dB per connector, and there are 4 connectors. Is the link feasible?

Step 1: Calculate cable loss: 40 km × 0.2 dB/km = 8 dB

Step 2: Calculate connector loss: 4 × 0.5 dB = 2 dB

Step 3: Total loss = 8 + 2 = 10 dB

Step 4: Received power = +3 dBm - 10 dB = -7 dBm

Step 5: Compare with receiver sensitivity: -7 dBm > -28 dBm

Link is feasible! Margin = 21 dB (plenty of headroom)

Example 4: Satellite Propagation Delay

Calculate the round-trip time for a signal from Earth to a GEO satellite at 35,786 km altitude.

Given: d = 35,786 km = 35,786,000 m, c = 3×10⁸ m/s

Step 1: One-way delay = d/c = 35,786,000 / 3×10⁸ ≈ 0.119 s = 119 ms

Step 2: Round-trip (Earth → Satellite → Earth) = 2 × 119 ms

RTT ≈ 238 ms (one-way: ~120 ms)

This explains why GEO satellite calls have noticeable delay. LEO satellites at 550 km have ~7 ms one-way.

Example 5: Wi-Fi vs Ethernet Speed

A user has a 100 Mbps Ethernet connection and a Wi-Fi 5 (802.11ac) access point rated at 1.3 Gbps. Why does the Ethernet feel faster?

Analysis:

• Wi-Fi 1.3 Gbps is the theoretical maximum (all streams, perfect conditions)

• Real Wi-Fi throughput: 200-400 Mbps typical

• Wi-Fi is shared medium — all devices share bandwidth

• Interference, walls, distance reduce actual speed

• Ethernet 100 Mbps is dedicated per port

• No interference on wired connection

Ethernet provides consistent 100 Mbps; Wi-Fi fluctuates and is shared.

Common Exam Mistakes & Warnings

Mistake 1: Confusing Speed with Range

Wi-Fi 6 is rated at 9.6 Gbps, but that's only at close range with ideal conditions. Real-world speed at 30m through walls may be 100-200 Mbps. Bluetooth 5.0 at 2 Mbps with 300m range doesn't mean it reaches 300m at 2 Mbps — range and speed are inversely related.

Mistake 2: Forgetting the 100m Limit for Copper

Twisted pair Ethernet is limited to 100 meters. Students often forget this and design networks with 200m cable runs. Always use switches or repeaters for distances > 100m with copper cables.

Mistake 3: Confusing Single-mode vs Multi-mode Fiber

Single-mode has a SMALLER core (8-10 μm) and LONGER range. Multi-mode has a LARGER core (50-62.5 μm) and SHORTER range. The counter-intuitive naming confuses students — remember: "single mode = single path = less dispersion = longer distance."

Mistake 4: Ignoring SNR in Shannon's Formula

SNR in Shannon's formula C = B×log₂(1+SNR) must be in LINEAR scale, not dB. If given SNR = 20 dB, convert first: SNR_linear = 10^(20/10) = 100, then use 100 in the formula.

Mistake 5: Satellite Latency is Bidirectional

GEO satellite delay is ~240 ms one-way, NOT round-trip. Round-trip is ~480 ms. This is why satellite Internet feels "slow" even though bandwidth may be high. LEO constellations (Starlink) reduce this to ~20-40 ms RTT.

Mistake 6: Microwave Requires Line-of-Sight

Microwave signals travel in straight lines. You cannot send microwave signals "around" mountains or through buildings. Each tower must "see" the next tower. Curvature of Earth limits tower spacing to ~50 km.

Mistake 7: Fiber is NOT Immune to Physical Damage

While fiber is immune to EMI, it is physically fragile. Bending a fiber cable too tightly causes signal loss (macro-bending loss). Minimum bend radius must be maintained during installation.

Mistake 8: Coaxial is NOT Obsolete

While rarely used for LANs anymore, coaxial is still widely used for cable TV (DOCSIS), satellite feeds (RG-6), and cable modems. Don't say "coaxial is never used" in exams.
