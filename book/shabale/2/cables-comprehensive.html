Coaxial Cable — Structure

Coaxial cable is a copper-based transmission medium with a concentric (coaxial) design. It consists of multiple layers arranged around a common central axis, providing excellent shielding against interference.

Structure (inside → outside):
1. Center Conductor (solid copper or stranded)
2. Dielectric Insulator (polyethylene or PTFE)
3. Metallic Shield (braided copper or foil)
4. Outer Jacket (PVC or PE)

Coaxial Cable — Impedance

Characteristic impedance (Z₀) is the most critical electrical property of coaxial cable. It depends on the geometry and dielectric material.

Impedance Formula:
Z₀ = (138 / √ε) × log₁₀(D/d)
Where: ε = dielectric constant, D = inner diameter of shield, d = diameter of center conductor

50Ω Cable

Used for: Data/Ethernet

Optimized for power handling

Used in early Ethernet (10BASE2, 10BASE5), amateur radio, military communications.

75Ω Cable

Used for: Video/TV

Optimized for low attenuation

Used in cable TV (CATV), satellite TV, broadband internet, video distribution.

52Ω Cable

Used for: Test Equipment

Compromise impedance

Specialized applications in RF test equipment and measurement systems.

93Ω Cable

Used for: Legacy Systems

RG-62 type

Used in some older IBM mainframe networks (ARCnet).

⚠ Never mix 50Ω and 75Ω cables in the same system. Impedance mismatch causes signal reflections and power loss.

Advantages & Disadvantages

Advantages

• Excellent shielding against EMI

• Higher bandwidth than twisted pair (up to 1 GHz)

• Longer distances than UTP

• Lower signal leakage

• Good for high-frequency signals

Disadvantages

• Expensive compared to twisted pair

• Difficult to install and bend

• Single cable failure can bring down network (bus topology)

• Heavier and bulkier

• Being replaced by twisted pair and fiber

Twisted Pair — Structure

Twisted pair is the most common type of copper cabling in modern networks. It consists of pairs of insulated copper wires twisted together to reduce electromagnetic interference.

Basic Structure:
Each pair = 2 insulated copper conductors twisted together
A standard Ethernet cable contains 4 pairs (8 wires total). The twisting cancels out EMI and reduces crosstalk between pairs.

⚠ Each pair is twisted at a slightly different rate (twists per inch) to minimize crosstalk between adjacent pairs.

STP vs UTP

UTP (Unshielded Twisted Pair)

Most common cable type

• No additional shielding around pairs

• Relies solely on twisting for noise rejection

• Cheaper, more flexible, easier to install

• Standard for office/LAN installations

• Categories: Cat 3 through Cat 8

STP (Shielded Twisted Pair)

Enhanced EMI protection

• Foil or braided shield around each pair

• Overall foil/braid shield around all pairs

• Better noise rejection than UTP

• Used in industrial environments

• More expensive, harder to install properly

STP Shielding Types:
FTP — overall Foil shield (no individual pair shields)
S/UTP — overall Braid shield (no individual pair shields)
S/FTP — overall Braid + individual pair Foil shields
F/UTP — overall Foil + no individual pair shields
S = braid shield, F = foil shield, U = unshielded pairs

Advantages & Disadvantages

Advantages

• Inexpensive and widely available

• Easy to install and terminate

• Flexible, works in tight spaces

• Supports high bandwidth (Cat 6a: 500 MHz)

• Uses standard RJ-45 connectors

Disadvantages

• Limited to 100m distance

• Susceptible to EMI at high frequencies

• Lower bandwidth than fiber optic

• Crosstalk issues at higher categories

RJ-45 Connector

RJ-45 (Registered Jack 45) is the standard connector for Ethernet over twisted pair. It has 8 positions and 8 contacts (8P8C).

Structure

• 8 pins (8P8C configuration)

• Plastic body with locking tab

• Gold-plated contacts

• Used with Cat 5e, Cat 6, Cat 6a cables

Wiring

• T-568A or T-568B pinout

• Pins 1,2: Transmit (Tx)

• Pins 3,6: Receive (Rx)

• Pins 4,5,7,8: PoE/unused

Applications

• 10/100/1000 Mbps Ethernet

• 10GBASE-T (Cat 6a)

• Power over Ethernet (PoE)

• LAN connections

Key Facts

• Max cable length: 100m

• Click when properly inserted

• Requires RJ-45 crimping tool

• Different from RJ-11 (phone)

BNC Connector

BNC (Bayonet Neill-Concelman) is a quick-connect RF connector used with coaxial cables. It uses a bayonet-style lock mechanism.

Structure

• Bayonet locking mechanism

• Center pin + outer shield contact

• Twist-lock connection

• Available in 50Ω and 75Ω versions

Components

• BNC Plug (male) — on cable

• BNC Jack (female) — on device

• BNC T-connector — splits signal

• BNC Terminator — terminates bus

Applications

• 10BASE2 Ethernet (Thinnet)

• Video signals (CCTV)

• RF test equipment

• Amateur radio

10BASE2 Usage

• T-connector at each device

• 50Ω terminators at cable ends

• Max 30 devices per segment

• Min 0.5m between T-connectors

⚠ BNC connectors are NOT the same as F-type connectors used for cable TV, though both are used with coaxial cable.

Fiber Optic Connectors — ST and SC

ST (Straight Tip)

Bayonet-style fiber connector

• Round, bayonet lock (like BNC)

• 2.5mm ceramic ferrule

• Twist to lock mechanism

• Common in older LAN installations

• Good for multimode fiber

• Less common in new installations

SC (Subscriber Connector)

Push-pull fiber connector

• Square, snap-in design

• 2.5mm ceramic ferrule

• Push to connect, pull to disconnect

• More popular than ST in modern installs

• Works with both multimode and single-mode

• Higher density deployments

Other Fiber Connectors:
LC — Small form factor, most popular today
MT-RJ — Small, snap-in, two fibers per connector
FC — Screw-on, used in test equipment
MTP/MPO — Multi-fiber, used in data centers

Worked Examples — Cable Selection Scenarios

Example 1: Office LAN Installation

Scenario: A company needs to connect 50 workstations in a single floor office. The longest cable run is 85 meters. They need Gigabit Ethernet (1 Gbps) and want the most cost-effective solution.

Analysis:

• Distance 85m < 100m limit → twisted pair is acceptable

• Gigabit Ethernet required → minimum Cat 5e

• Office environment → no excessive EMI

• Cost-effective → UTP preferred over STP

Recommendation:
Cat 5e UTP (or Cat 6 for future-proofing)
Use T-568B standard with RJ-45 connectors on both ends. Cat 6 provides headroom for potential 10G upgrade up to 55m.
Example 2: Factory Floor Network

Scenario: A manufacturing plant needs to connect machines on a factory floor with heavy electrical equipment. There are large motors, welding machines, and high-voltage power lines creating significant EMI.

Analysis:

• High EMI environment → shielding is critical

• Industrial setting → rugged connectors needed

• Distance may vary, but 100m limit applies

• Reliability is paramount

Recommendation:
Cat 6a STP with industrial-grade connectors
S/FTP (overall braid + individual foil shields) provides maximum EMI protection. Consider fiber optic for very noisy areas or distances >100m.
⚠ In extreme EMI environments, fiber optic is always the best choice since it is completely immune to electromagnetic interference.
Example 3: Cable TV and Broadband Setup

Scenario: A residential home needs cable TV and broadband internet. The service provider delivers signals through a coaxial cable that splits to 4 TVs and a cable modem. The longest run from the splitter to a TV is 30 meters.

Analysis:

• Cable TV/broadband → 75Ω impedance required

• Video signals → need low attenuation

• Residential → standard RG-6 is sufficient

• 30m run → within acceptable limits for RG-6

Recommendation:
RG-6 75Ω coaxial with F-type connectors
Use RG-6 for all runs up to 30m. For runs longer than 50m, use RG-11 (lower attenuation). F-type connectors are standard for cable TV. Use a 75Ω splitter to maintain impedance.
Example 4: Data Center Server Connection

Scenario: A data center needs to connect servers to top-of-rack switches at 10 Gbps. Cable runs are short (2-5 meters) but need maximum density and performance. The environment has good EMI control (air-conditioned room).

Analysis:

• 10 Gbps required → Cat 6a minimum for copper

• Short runs (2-5m) → copper or fiber both viable

• High density → small form factor preferred

• Clean environment → UTP acceptable

Recommendation:
Cat 6a UTP with RJ-45 OR OM3/OM4 fiber with LC connectors
Copper: Cat 6a supports 10GBASE-T up to 100m, cost-effective. Fiber: OM3/OM4 with LC connectors provides higher density, lower latency, and future scalability to 40/100 Gbps.
Example 5: Legacy Network Upgrade

Scenario: A university has an old Ethernet network using 10BASE2 (Thinnet) with BNC connectors. They need to upgrade to Gigabit Ethernet for 200 classrooms. Budget is limited and existing cable conduits can accommodate new cables.

Analysis:

• Current: 10BASE2 (coaxial, BNC, bus topology)

• Target: 1 Gbps Ethernet

• Bus topology → must switch to star topology

• 100m limit per run → most classrooms within range

Recommendation:
Cat 5e or Cat 6 UTP with RJ-45, star topology with switches
Remove all coaxial/BNC infrastructure. Install Cat 5e (minimum) or Cat 6 UTP cables from each classroom to a central wiring closet with Ethernet switches. Use RJ-45 connectors. This provides 100× speed improvement and modern star topology.
⚠ The old BNC terminators and T-connectors must be removed. Mixing old coaxial with new twisted pair infrastructure will not work.

Cable Selection Decision Guide

Step 1: Check distance
> 100m → Use fiber optic
≤ 100m → Copper cable possible
Step 2: Check EMI environment
High EMI → STP or Fiber
Low EMI → UTP is fine
Step 3: Check speed requirements
≤ 1 Gbps → Cat 5e/6 UTP
10 Gbps → Cat 6a/7 UTP or Fiber
> 10 Gbps → Fiber only
Step 4: Check application
Video/CATV → 75Ω Coaxial (RG-6)
Data/LAN → Twisted Pair or Fiber
Industrial → STP or Fiber

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1

Exceeding 100m with UTP

Signals degrade beyond 100m

Mistake 2

Mixing 50Ω and 75Ω coax

Causes reflections and loss

Mistake 3

Using UTP in high EMI areas

Use STP or fiber instead

Mistake 4

Forgetting 10BASE2 terminators

Bus needs 50Ω terminators at both ends
