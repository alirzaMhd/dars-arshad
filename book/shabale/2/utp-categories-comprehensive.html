Physical Layer — Complete Guide for Exam

What is UTP Cable?

Unshielded Twisted Pair (UTP) is the most widely used type of copper cabling in local area networks. It consists of pairs of insulated copper wires twisted together to reduce electromagnetic interference (EMI) and crosstalk.

Structure:
4 pairs × 2 wires per pair = 8 conductors
Each pair is twisted at a different rate to minimize crosstalk between pairs. Standard connectors: RJ-45 (8P8C).
Why Twisting?
Twisting cancels out external electromagnetic interference
When external noise hits a twisted pair, it affects both wires equally. Differential signaling at the receiver subtracts the common noise, recovering the original signal.

Key Concepts

Bandwidth vs Speed

Bandwidth = frequency range (MHz)

Speed = data rate (Mbps/Gbps)

Higher bandwidth enables higher data rates

Category vs Class

Category = specific cable spec (Cat 5e)

Class = performance level (Class D)

One class may cover multiple categories

Shielding Types

UTP = Unshielded Twisted Pair

FTP = Foil Twisted Pair

STP = Shielded Twisted Pair

S/FTP = Shielded + Foil (Cat 7)

Distance Limit

100 meters = maximum run length

90m horizontal + 10m patch cables

Exceeding causes signal degradation

Why Categories Matter

⚠ Using the wrong category cable can create a network bottleneck. Even if your switches support 1 Gbps, Cat 3 cable limits you to 10 Mbps!

Network Performance

Cable category determines the maximum throughput of your network link.

Future-Proofing

Installing higher category cables avoids costly re-cabling when upgrading equipment.

Standards Compliance

Many regulations and certifications require minimum cable categories.

Cost vs Benefit

Higher categories cost more but provide better performance and reliability.

Cat 5e (enhanced) is the most widely deployed cable category today. It supports Gigabit Ethernet (1000BASE-T) using all 4 pairs.

Key Improvements Over Cat 5:
Better crosstalk specs → Gigabit support
Cat 5e has stricter specifications for Near-End Crosstalk (NEXT) and Return Loss compared to Cat 5, enabling full-duplex Gigabit over all 4 pairs.

Supported Standards

• 100BASE-TX (100 Mbps)

• 1000BASE-T (1 Gbps)

Cat 6 supports 10GBASE-T (10 Gigabit Ethernet) but only up to 55 meters. For full 100m 10G, use Cat 6a.

Key Features:
2.5× bandwidth of Cat 5e + better shielding
Cat 6 has tighter pair twisting, better insulation, and optionally a separator spline to reduce crosstalk. At 250 MHz, it provides headroom for 10 Gbps at shorter distances.

Supported Standards

• All Cat 5e standards

• 10GBASE-T (up to 55m)

• 2.5GBASE-T, 5GBASE-T

Limitations

• 10G limited to 55m

• Alien crosstalk issues at 10G

• Heavier and stiffer than Cat 5e

⚠ Cat 6 can do 10 Gbps, but only up to 55m. For full 100m runs at 10G, you need Cat 6a.

Cat 7 is the first category to require individual pair shielding (S/FTP). Each pair is wrapped in foil, plus an overall braid shield, providing 600 MHz bandwidth.

Shielding Structure (S/FTP):
Individual foil per pair + overall braided shield
This dual shielding virtually eliminates alien crosstalk and provides excellent EMI protection. Uses GG45 or TERA connectors (backward compatible with RJ-45).

Key Features

• 600 MHz bandwidth

• 10 Gbps at full 100m

• Individual pair foil shielding

• Overall braided shield

• GG45/TERA connectors

Best For

• Data centers

• High-speed backbone links

• EMI-heavy environments

• Future-proof installations

Higher cost but maximum performance

⚠ Cat 7 uses different connectors (GG45/TERA) by default. Ensure compatibility with your equipment before purchasing.

Cat 8 provides 40 Gigabit Ethernet at 2000 MHz bandwidth, but is limited to 30 meters. Designed exclusively for data center short-run applications.

Cat 8 uses S/FTP shielding like Cat 7 and supports 25GBASE-T and 40GBASE-T. The 30m limit makes it suitable only for short patch cables in data centers.

Decision Flowchart

Step 1: What speed do you need?

• 10 Mbps or voice only → Cat 3 (legacy) or Cat 5e (minimum recommended)

• 100 Mbps → Cat 5e (minimum) or Cat 6

• 1 Gbps → Cat 5e (minimum) or Cat 6

• 10 Gbps → Cat 6 (up to 55m) or Cat 6a (full 100m)

• 40-100 Gbps → Cat 8 (30m) or Cat 7/8

Step 2: What is your budget?

• Lowest cost → Cat 5e (~$0.30/m)

• Moderate cost → Cat 6 (~$0.50/m)

• Higher cost → Cat 6a (~$0.80/m)

• Premium → Cat 7 (~$1.50/m)

Step 3: What is your environment?

• Office/home → Cat 5e or Cat 6

• Industrial/EMI heavy → Cat 6a or Cat 7

• Data center → Cat 6a, Cat 7, or Cat 8

Warning: Common Selection Mistakes

Mistake 1

Using Cat 5 for Gigabit networks

Cat 5 maxes at 100 Mbps. Use Cat 5e minimum.

Mistake 2

Buying Cat 7 for runs over 100m

No copper category exceeds 100m. Use fiber for longer runs.

Mistake 3

Assuming Cat 6 = 10G at 100m

Cat 6 only does 10G at 55m. Use Cat 6a for 100m.

Mistake 4

Mixing categories in a link

A link is only as good as its weakest cable segment.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Home Office Network Upgrade

Scenario: You are setting up a home office with a Gigabit router and need to connect 3 computers. You want the network to support video conferencing and file transfers. Budget is moderate.

Analysis:
Requirement: 1 Gbps, ~20m runs, moderate budget
Cat 5e supports 1 Gbps at 100m and is the cheapest option. Cat 6 adds headroom and better future-proofing for ~40% more cost.

✓ Best Choice: Cat 6

Reason: Cat 6 provides Gigabit with extra bandwidth headroom (250 MHz vs 100 MHz). It's the sweet spot for home use — affordable, fast, and ready for potential 2.5G/5G upgrades.

Example 2: Office VoIP Deployment

Scenario: A company is deploying IP phones across 3 floors. Each floor has 50 workstations with phones daisy-chained (phone → PC). Maximum cable run: 80m. Current network is Gigabit.

Analysis:
VoIP needs ~100 Kbps per call + Gigabit backbone
VoIP itself needs minimal bandwidth, but the PC connection behind each phone needs Gigabit. All 4 pairs are used by 1000BASE-T.

✓ Best Choice: Cat 5e (minimum) or Cat 6

Reason: Cat 5e fully supports Gigabit at 80m and is the industry standard for office voice/data. Cat 6 is preferred for new installations as it provides margin for future upgrades. Cat 3 is NOT suitable — it only supports 10 Mbps.

Example 3: Data Center Server Room

Scenario: A data center needs to connect top-of-rack switches to servers. Run distances: 15-25m. Required speed: 10 Gbps minimum, 40 Gbps preferred. EMI from nearby power equipment.

Analysis:
Short runs + 10-40G + EMI → need shielding
Cat 6a supports 10G at 100m. Cat 7 supports 10G at 100m with better shielding. Cat 8 supports 40G at 30m — perfect for these distances.

✓ Best Choice: Cat 8 (for 40G) or Cat 7 (for 10G with EMI protection)

Reason: Cat 8 provides 40Gbps at up to 30m — ideal for short data center runs. If 10G is sufficient, Cat 7's S/FTP shielding handles the EMI better than Cat 6a UTP. Cat 6 would work at 10G but lacks the shielding for this EMI-heavy environment.

Example 4: Campus Backbone Link

Scenario: Two buildings on a campus need to be connected. Distance: 90m between buildings. Required speed: 10 Gbps. The cable will run through an underground conduit with power cables.

Analysis:
90m run + 10G + EMI from power cables
Cat 6 only does 10G at 55m — too short. Cat 6a does 10G at 100m. Cat 7 does 10G at 100m with superior EMI protection.

✓ Best Choice: Cat 6a or Cat 7

Reason: Cat 6a supports 10G at full 100m, which covers the 90m run. However, since the cable runs alongside power cables (EMI source), Cat 7's S/FTP shielding is the safer choice. Fiber optic would be even better for this scenario.

Example 5: Budget-Constrained School Lab

Scenario: A school needs to wire a computer lab with 30 PCs. Current network is 100 Mbps Fast Ethernet. Budget is very tight. The lab will be used for basic internet browsing and document editing.

Analysis:
100 Mbps needs only Cat 5, but Cat 5e is minimum recommended
Cat 5 supports 100 Mbps at 100m. Cat 5e supports 1 Gbps at 100m. Price difference is minimal (~$0.10/m more for Cat 5e).

✓ Best Choice: Cat 5e

Reason: Even though Cat 5 technically supports 100 Mbps, it is essentially obsolete. Cat 5e costs only slightly more and supports Gigabit, providing future-proofing if the school upgrades to a Gigabit switch. Cat 3 would be too slow (10 Mbps max). Always go with Cat 5e as the absolute minimum.

Warning: Common Mistakes

Mistake 1: "Cat 5 is fine for my network"

Cat 5 maxes at 100 Mbps. Modern networks need at least Cat 5e for Gigabit.

Mistake 2: "I bought Cat 7, so I can go 200m"

No copper category exceeds 100m. For longer runs, use fiber.

Mistake 3: "Cat 6 = 10G everywhere"

Cat 6 10G is limited to 55m. Beyond that, use Cat 6a.

Mistake 4: "Shielding is always better"

UTP is fine for most offices. Shielded cable needs proper grounding or it's worse than UTP.

Mistake 5: "Cheapest cable works"

Cheap, non-certified cables may not meet specs. Always buy TIA/EIA certified cable.

Mistake 6: "All Cat 6 is the same"

Cat 6 vs Cat 6a matters. Check bandwidth (250 vs 500 MHz) and 10G distance support.
