SIGNAL PROPERTIES

Physical Layer — Complete Guide for 20/20 Exam

1. Amplitude

The strength or height of a signal. It represents the maximum displacement of the wave from its zero (rest) position.

What it measures:
Peak voltage (V) or power (W)
How "strong" or "loud" the signal is. Higher amplitude = stronger signal.

High Amplitude

• Strong signal

• Travels longer distance

• More power consumed

Low Amplitude

• Weak signal

• Shorter range

• Less power consumed

Warning: Increasing amplitude does NOT increase data rate. It only increases the signal's ability to travel farther without being lost in noise.

2. Frequency

The number of complete cycles per second. Measured in Hertz (Hz).

Definition:
f = number of cycles / time in seconds
Unit: Hertz (Hz). 1 Hz = 1 cycle per second. Common: kHz (10³), MHz (10⁶), GHz (10⁹)
Frequency Examples

• Human voice: 300 Hz – 3,400 Hz

• FM radio: 88 MHz – 108 MHz

• Wi-Fi 2.4 GHz: 2.4 GHz = 2.4 × 10⁹ Hz

• Visible light: 400 THz – 800 THz

Warning: Higher frequency does NOT automatically mean faster data. The data rate depends on both frequency AND the encoding scheme used.

3. Wavelength (λ)

The physical distance between two consecutive identical points on a wave (e.g., peak to peak, or trough to trough).

Formula:
λ = C / f
λ = wavelength (meters), C = speed of light (3 × 10⁸ m/s), f = frequency (Hz)

Key Fact

Wavelength and frequency are inversely proportional.

• Higher frequency → shorter wavelength

• Lower frequency → longer wavelength

4. Phase

The position of a point in a cycle at a given moment, measured in degrees (0° to 360°) or radians (0 to 2π).

Phase Shift:
Phase shift = (time delay / period) × 360°
Two waves can have the same amplitude and frequency but different phases.

0° (In Phase)

∿∿∿∿∿∿

Waves aligned perfectly

180° (Out of Phase)

∿∿∿∿∿∿ (inverted)

Waves are mirror images — they cancel out!

90° (Quadrature)

∿∿∿∿∿∿ (shifted)

Waves are perpendicular in time

Warning: Phase cancellation is real! When two identical signals are 180° out of phase, they completely cancel each other. This is used in noise-cancelling headphones.

5. Period (T)

The time for one complete cycle of a wave. It is the reciprocal of frequency.

Formula:
T = 1 / f
T = period (seconds), f = frequency (Hz). They are inversely proportional.
Period Examples

• f = 50 Hz → T = 1/50 = 0.02 s = 20 ms

• f = 1 GHz → T = 1/(10⁹) = 1 ns

• f = 2.4 GHz → T = 1/(2.4×10⁹) = 0.417 ns

Core Relationships

All signal properties are interconnected. Understanding these relationships is essential.

Relationship 1 — Frequency and Period:
f = 1/T   |   T = 1/f
Frequency and period are reciprocals. Double the frequency = half the period.
Relationship 2 — Wavelength and Frequency:
λ = C / f   |   f = C / λ   |   C = λ × f
C = speed of light = 3 × 10⁸ m/s. Wavelength and frequency are inversely proportional.
Relationship 3 — Wavelength and Period:
λ = C × T
Since T = 1/f, substituting gives λ = C × T. Longer period = longer wavelength.

Visual Relationships

If you increase... Frequency (f) Period (T) Wavelength (λ)
Frequency ↑ — ↓ decreases ↓ decreases
Period ↑ ↓ decreases — ↑ increases
Wavelength ↑ ↓ decreases ↑ increases —
Critical Formula Chain:
Know this by heart: λ = C / f = C × T
If you know ANY one of (λ, f, T), you can find the other two using C = 3 × 10⁸ m/s.

Example 1: Calculate Wavelength

Problem

A signal has a frequency of 5 GHz. What is its wavelength?

Step 1: Write the formula: λ = C / f
Step 2: Convert: 5 GHz = 5 × 10⁹ Hz
Step 3: Substitute: λ = (3 × 10⁸) / (5 × 10⁹)
Step 4: Calculate: λ = 0.06 m = 6 cm

Example 2: Calculate Frequency

Problem

A radio wave has a wavelength of 2 meters. What is its frequency?

Step 1: Write the formula: f = C / λ
Step 2: Substitute: f = (3 × 10⁸) / 2
Step 3: Calculate: f = 1.5 × 10⁸ Hz = 150 MHz
This falls in the VHF band used for FM radio and TV broadcasts.

Example 3: Calculate Period

Problem

A Wi-Fi router operates at 2.4 GHz. What is the period of the signal?

Step 1: Write the formula: T = 1 / f
Step 2: Convert: 2.4 GHz = 2.4 × 10⁹ Hz
Step 3: Substitute: T = 1 / (2.4 × 10⁹)
Step 4: Calculate: T = 4.167 × 10⁻¹⁰ s ≈ 0.417 ns
This means one complete cycle takes less than half a nanosecond!

Example 4: Wavelength from Period

Problem

A signal has a period of 10 ns. What is its wavelength?

Step 1: Method A: Find frequency first. f = 1/T = 1/(10 × 10⁻⁹) = 10⁸ Hz = 100 MHz
Step 2: Then: λ = C / f = (3 × 10⁸) / (10⁸) = 3 meters
Alternative: Method B: Use λ = C × T directly = (3 × 10⁸) × (10 × 10⁻⁹) = 3 meters
Both methods give the same answer. Use whichever feels more natural.

Example 5: Visible Light Spectrum

Problem

Red light has a wavelength of approximately 700 nm. What is its frequency?

Step 1: Convert wavelength: 700 nm = 700 × 10⁻⁹ m = 7 × 10⁻⁷ m
Step 2: Write the formula: f = C / λ
Step 3: Substitute: f = (3 × 10⁸) / (7 × 10⁻⁷)
Step 4: Calculate: f ≈ 4.29 × 10¹⁴ Hz = 429 THz
Visible light frequencies are in the terahertz range — much higher than radio waves.

Frequency Bandwidth

The range of frequencies occupied by a signal or available for communication. It is the difference between the highest and lowest frequencies.

Formula:
Bandwidth = f_max − f_min
Bandwidth is measured in Hz. Wider bandwidth = more data can be transmitted.
Bandwidth Example

A channel uses frequencies from 100 MHz to 104 MHz.

Bandwidth = 104 MHz − 100 MHz = 4 MHz

Why Bandwidth Matters

Higher Bandwidth

↑ More data per second

• Faster downloads

• Better video quality

• Requires more spectrum

Lower Bandwidth

↓ Less data per second

• Slower speeds

• Lower quality

• Cheaper to deploy

Types of Bandwidth

Type Description Example
Baseband Signal uses entire channel; digital transmission Ethernet (100 Mbps)
Broadband Multiple signals share channel via frequency division Cable TV, DSL
Passband Band of frequencies used after modulation Wi-Fi channels

Common Communication Bands

Exam Tip: Bandwidth is NOT the same as frequency. Bandwidth is a RANGE (difference), while frequency is a single value. A 5 GHz signal has a frequency of 5 GHz, but its bandwidth depends on how much spectrum it occupies.

Nyquist & Shannon Limits

Nyquist Theorem (Noiseless Channel):
Max Data Rate = 2 × B × log₂(L)
B = bandwidth (Hz), L = number of signal levels
Shannon's Theorem (Noisy Channel):
Max Data Rate = B × log₂(1 + SNR)
B = bandwidth (Hz), SNR = signal-to-noise ratio (linear, not dB)
These theorems show that both bandwidth and signal quality (SNR) affect maximum data rate.

Key Exam Warnings

1. Unit Conversions: Always convert GHz to Hz (×10⁹), MHz to Hz (×10⁶), nm to m (×10⁻⁹) before using formulas. Forgetting this is the #1 mistake.
2. Speed of Light: In vacuum, C = 3 × 10⁸ m/s. In copper cable, signals travel at ~2/3 C. In fiber, ~2/3 C. The exam usually assumes vacuum unless stated otherwise.
3. Phase ≠ Frequency: Two signals can have the same frequency but different phases. Phase describes WHERE in the cycle a signal is; frequency describes HOW FAST it cycles.
4. Bandwidth ≠ Frequency: Bandwidth is a RANGE. A signal centered at 5 GHz with 20 MHz bandwidth occupies 4.99 GHz to 5.01 GHz.
5. Inverse Relationships: Frequency ↑ → Period ↓ and Wavelength ↓. Always. This is the most tested concept.
