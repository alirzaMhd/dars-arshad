DISTORTION IN SIGNAL TRANSMISSION

Physical Layer — Complete Guide for Exam

What is Distortion?

Distortion is the alteration or deformation of a signal's waveform during transmission through a communication channel. Unlike noise (which adds unwanted energy), distortion changes the shape of the original signal itself.

Definition:
Distortion = unwanted change in signal waveform during transmission
The received signal differs from the transmitted signal not due to added noise, but due to changes in amplitude, phase, or timing of different frequency components.
⚠ Distortion is different from noise: Noise ADDS unwanted energy to the signal, while distortion CHANGES the shape of the signal itself.

Why Distortion Matters

Signal Degradation

The original waveform becomes unrecognizable, making it impossible to correctly interpret the transmitted data.

Intersymbol Interference (ISI)

Distorted signals spread in time, causing adjacent symbols to overlap and interfere with each other.

Increased Error Rate

Receiver misinterprets distorted bits, leading to data corruption and retransmissions.

Reduced Bandwidth

Distortion limits the effective usable bandwidth of a channel, reducing data transmission capacity.

Key Properties of Distortion

Frequency Dependent

Different frequencies are affected differently, causing waveform deformation.

Channel Specific

Each channel has unique distortion characteristics based on its physical properties.

Distance Accumulates

Distortion increases with transmission distance as signal travels through more channel.

Correctable

Unlike noise, distortion can often be reversed using equalization techniques.

Delay Distortion (Group Delay Distortion)

Delay distortion occurs in guided media (cables) when different frequency components of a signal travel at different speeds through the medium. This causes the signal to spread out in time.

Group Delay:
τ_g = -dφ/dω
Where τ_g = group delay (seconds), φ = phase shift, ω = angular frequency. Ideal: constant group delay across all frequencies.

How Delay Distortion Works

In Baseband Transmission

Different frequency components of a digital pulse travel at different velocities. The pulse spreads out in time, causing it to overlap with adjacent pulses.

In Coaxial Cable

Higher frequencies travel slower than lower frequencies due to dielectric properties. This creates group delay variation.

⚠ Delay distortion is the PRIMARY limitation for data rates in twisted-pair and coaxial cables. It causes Intersymbol Interference (ISI) at high speeds.

Chromatic Dispersion (in Fiber Optics)

Chromatic distortion (chromatic dispersion) occurs in optical fibers when different wavelengths (colors) of light travel at different speeds through the fiber.

Chromatic Dispersion:
D = D_material + D_waveguide
Total dispersion = Material dispersion + Waveguide dispersion. Measured in ps/(nm·km).

Two Components of Chromatic Dispersion

Material Dispersion

The refractive index of glass varies with wavelength. Different wavelengths travel at different speeds through the fiber material.

Waveguide Dispersion

The propagation characteristics depend on the waveguide structure. Different wavelengths have different confinement in core/cladding.

⚠ Chromatic dispersion limits both the distance and data rate of fiber optic systems. It is the main reason for using single-mode fiber in long-distance communications.

Modal Dispersion (Intermodal Dispersion)

Modal distortion occurs in multimode fiber when different modes (paths) of light travel different distances through the fiber, arriving at different times.

Multimode Step-Index

Worst distortion. Light rays take different paths, with higher-order modes traveling much longer distances.

Multimode Graded-Index

Reduced distortion. Gradual refractive index change equalizes travel times of different modes.

Single-Mode Fiber

No modal distortion. Only one mode propagates, eliminating intermodal delay differences.

Amplitude Distortion

Amplitude distortion occurs when different frequency components of a signal are attenuated by different amounts as they pass through the channel.

Frequency Response:
H(f) = Output(f) / Input(f)
If |H(f)| is not constant across the signal bandwidth, amplitude distortion occurs.

Phase Distortion

Phase distortion occurs when different frequency components experience different phase shifts through the channel, altering the waveform shape.

Linear Phase (No Distortion):
φ(f) = -2πfτ (constant group delay)
All frequencies experience the same time delay. The waveform shape is preserved.

Fundamental Cause: Frequency-Dependent Propagation

The root cause of most distortion is that different frequency components travel at different speeds through the transmission medium.

Phase Velocity:
v_p = c / n(f)
Where v_p = phase velocity, c = speed of light, n(f) = frequency-dependent refractive index. Since n varies with f, different frequencies travel at different speeds.
⚠ This is the single most important concept: Distortion happens because the medium treats different frequencies differently.

Causes in Copper Cables

Capacitive Effects

Distributed capacitance between conductors causes frequency-dependent phase shifts. Higher frequencies see more capacitive reactance.

Inductive Effects

Distributed inductance in the cable creates frequency-dependent impedance, affecting signal propagation speed.

Skin Effect

At high frequencies, current flows only near the conductor surface, increasing effective resistance and affecting propagation.

Dielectric Absorption

The insulating material absorbs energy differently at different frequencies, causing amplitude and phase distortion.

Causes in Fiber Optics

Material Dispersion

Glass refractive index varies with wavelength. Different wavelengths travel at different speeds through the fiber.

Waveguide Dispersion

Fiber geometry causes different wavelengths to have different propagation constants and confinement.

Modal Dispersion

In multimode fiber, different modes take different paths, arriving at different times.

Polarization Mode Dispersion

Different polarization states travel at slightly different speeds due to fiber asymmetry.

Channel Non-Ideality

No real channel has a perfectly flat frequency response. All channels introduce some level of distortion:

Non-Flat Magnitude Response

Some frequencies are amplified more than others, causing amplitude distortion.

Non-Linear Phase Response

Phase shift doesn't scale linearly with frequency, causing delay distortion.

Multipath Propagation

Signal takes multiple paths with different delays, causing constructive/destructive interference.

Bandwidth Limitations

Channel cannot pass all frequencies equally, cutting off high-frequency components.

Effects on Data Integrity

Intersymbol Interference (ISI)

Pulses spread and overlap, making it impossible to distinguish individual bits. Primary limiting factor for data rate.

Eye Diagram Closure

The eye opening in the eye diagram closes, indicating increased bit error rate and reduced noise margin.

Timing Recovery Errors

Distorted signals make it difficult for the receiver to synchronize with the transmitter clock.

Increased BER

Bit error rate increases as distortion makes symbols harder to distinguish correctly.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Pulse Spreading in Coaxial Cable

Problem: A 10 MHz digital signal is transmitted through a 2 km coaxial cable. The group delay variation is 5 ns/kHz. Calculate the maximum pulse spreading and determine if ISI will occur for a 1 Mbps data rate.

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate total group delay variation:

Total group delay variation:
Δτ = Group delay variation × Cable length × Bandwidth
Δτ = 5 ns/kHz × 2 km × 10 MHz
Δτ = 5 × 10⁻⁹ × 2 × 10³ × 10 × 10⁶
Δτ = 100 ns

Step 2: Calculate bit period for 1 Mbps:

Bit period:
T_bit = 1 / Data rate = 1 / 10⁶ = 1 μs = 1000 ns

Step 3: Compare pulse spreading to bit period:

ISI Check:
Δτ / T_bit = 100 / 1000 = 0.1 (10%)
Pulse spreading is 10% of bit period. ISI is minimal, but will become significant at higher data rates.
Example 2: Chromatic Dispersion in Fiber

Problem: A single-mode fiber has a chromatic dispersion coefficient of D = 17 ps/(nm·km). If the light source has a spectral width of 0.1 nm and the fiber is 50 km long, calculate the pulse broadening. Can this fiber support a 1 Gbps data rate?

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate pulse broadening:

Pulse broadening:
Δτ = D × Δλ × L
Δτ = 17 ps/(nm·km) × 0.1 nm × 50 km
Δτ = 17 × 0.1 × 50 = 85 ps

Step 2: Calculate bit period for 1 Gbps:

Bit period:
T_bit = 1 / 10⁹ = 1 ns = 1000 ps

Step 3: Check if data rate is supported:

Dispersion Limit:
Δτ / T_bit = 85 / 1000 = 0.085 (8.5%)
Pulse spreading is 8.5% of bit period. This is acceptable (typically <25% is fine). Yes, 1 Gbps is supported over 50 km.
Example 3: Modal Dispersion in Multimode Fiber

Problem: A step-index multimode fiber has a core refractive index n₁ = 1.48 and cladding index n₂ = 1.46. The fiber is 1 km long. Calculate the maximum intermodal delay and the maximum data rate.

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate numerical aperture and maximum delay:

Maximum intermodal delay:
Δτ = (L × n₁ / c) × (n₁ - n₂) / n₂
Δτ = (1000 × 1.48 / 3×10⁸) × (1.48 - 1.46) / 1.46
Δτ = (4.933 × 10⁻⁶) × 0.0137
Δτ = 67.6 ns/km

Step 2: Calculate maximum data rate (using rule of thumb: T_bit ≥ 2×Δτ):

Maximum data rate:
Data rate ≤ 1 / (2 × Δτ) = 1 / (2 × 67.6 × 10⁻⁹)
Data rate ≤ 7.4 Mbps

This is why step-index multimode fiber is limited to low data rates. Graded-index fiber reduces this by ~100×.

Example 4: Dispersion-Limited Distance

Problem: A laser transmitter with 0.1 nm spectral width sends data through fiber with D = 17 ps/(nm·km). If the system requires Δτ < 0.25 × T_bit for 2.5 Gbps, what is the maximum transmission distance?

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate allowable pulse broadening:

Bit period:
T_bit = 1 / 2.5×10⁹ = 400 ps
Maximum allowed broadening:
Δτ_max = 0.25 × T_bit = 0.25 × 400 = 100 ps

Step 2: Calculate maximum distance:

From Δτ = D × Δλ × L:
L_max = Δτ_max / (D × Δλ)
L_max = 100 ps / (17 ps/(nm·km) × 0.1 nm)
L_max = 100 / 1.7 = 58.8 km

The system is limited to approximately 59 km at 2.5 Gbps with this source and fiber.

Example 5: Comparing Distortion in Different Media

Problem: Compare the maximum data rates achievable over 1 km for: (a) UTP cable with delay distortion limit of 50 ns/km, (b) Multimode fiber with modal dispersion of 20 ns/km, (c) Single-mode fiber with chromatic dispersion of 0.5 ns/km. Assume a 20% margin is required.

Solution:

Step 1: UTP cable:

UTP (copper):
T_bit ≥ Δτ / 0.2 = 50 ns / 0.2 = 250 ns
Max data rate = 1 / 250 ns = 4 Mbps

Step 2: Multimode fiber:

Multimode fiber:
T_bit ≥ 20 ns / 0.2 = 100 ns
Max data rate = 1 / 100 ns = 10 Mbps

Step 3: Single-mode fiber:

Single-mode fiber:
T_bit ≥ 0.5 ns / 0.2 = 2.5 ns
Max data rate = 1 / 2.5 ns = 400 Mbps

Conclusion: Single-mode fiber achieves 100× higher data rate than copper due to minimal distortion.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1

Confusing distortion with noise

Distortion changes shape, noise adds energy

Mistake 2

Forgetting modal dispersion in multimode fiber

Only single-mode eliminates modal effects

Mistake 3

Using wrong dispersion coefficient units

D is in ps/(nm·km), not ps/km

Mistake 4

Ignoring spectral width of light source

LED has wider spectrum than laser
