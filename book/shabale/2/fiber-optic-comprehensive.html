FIBER OPTIC CABLE

Physical Layer — Complete Guide for Exam

What is Fiber Optic Cable?

Fiber optic cable uses light pulses to transmit data through thin strands of glass or plastic. It is the fastest and most reliable transmission medium used in modern networks.

Definition:
Fiber Optic = Light-based data transmission through glass/plastic fibers
Information is encoded as light pulses (on/off) and guided through the fiber core by total internal reflection.

Innermost layer

• Glass or plastic material

Refractive index: n₁ (higher)

2. Cladding

Surrounds the core

• Reflects light back into core

• Lower refractive index than core

• Same material as core (doped)

• 125 μm diameter (standard)

Refractive index: n₂ (lower)

3. Buffer Coating

Protective plastic layer

• Protects from moisture/damage

• Adds mechanical strength

• Acrylate or silicone material

• 250 μm diameter

No optical function

4. Outer Jacket

Outermost protective layer

• PVC or LSZH material

• Provides environmental protection

• Color-coded by fiber type

• Various flame ratings

Yellow = Single-mode

Total Internal Reflection (TIR)

Total Internal Reflection is the principle that allows light to be trapped inside the fiber core and travel long distances with minimal loss.

How TIR Works:
Light in denser medium → strikes boundary at angle > critical angle → reflects back completely
No light escapes into the cladding. The signal bounces along the core until it reaches the receiver.
⚠ TIR only occurs when: (1) Light travels from denser to rarer medium (n₁ > n₂), and (2) Angle of incidence > critical angle.

Snell's Law

Snell's Law of Refraction:
n₁ × sin(θ₁) = n₂ × sin(θ₂)
Where n₁ = refractive index of core, n₂ = refractive index of cladding, θ₁ = angle of incidence, θ₂ = angle of refraction.
Alternative Form:
sin(θ₁) / sin(θ₂) = n₂ / n₁
The ratio of sines equals the inverse ratio of refractive indices.

Critical Angle (θc)

The critical angle is the angle of incidence above which total internal reflection occurs.

Critical Angle Formula:
θc = arcsin(n₂ / n₁)
When θ₁ = θc, the refracted ray travels along the boundary (θ₂ = 90°).
Derived from Snell's Law:
n₁ × sin(θc) = n₂ × sin(90°) = n₂ × 1
Therefore: sin(θc) = n₂ / n₁

Numerical Aperture (NA)

Numerical Aperture defines the light-gathering ability of the fiber — the maximum angle at which light can enter the fiber and still be guided.

Numerical Aperture Formula:
NA = sin(θa) = √(n₁² - n₂²)
Where θa = acceptance angle (half-angle), n₁ = core refractive index, n₂ = cladding refractive index.
Acceptance Angle:
θa = arcsin(NA) = arcsin(√(n₁² - n₂²))
Light entering within this cone is guided by TIR. Light outside this cone escapes into cladding.
Relative Refractive Index Difference:
Δ = (n₁² - n₂²) / (2 × n₁²) ≈ (n₁ - n₂) / n₁
Typical values: Δ = 0.01 to 0.03 for standard fibers.

Refraction vs Reflection

Refraction

• Light bends when entering new medium

• Depends on refractive indices

• Follows Snell's Law

• θ₁ ≠ θ₂ (unless n₁ = n₂)

Total Internal Reflection

• Light reflects back completely

• No light lost to cladding

• θ₁ = θr (angle of reflection)

• Only when θ₁ > θc

⚠ Common Exam Trap: n₁ (core) is ALWAYS greater than n₂ (cladding) for TIR to work. If n₂ > n₁, light refracts away from the boundary and escapes.

Fiber Optic Advantages

1. Extremely High Bandwidth

• 100+ Gbps per fiber

• Light carries far more data than electrical signals

• Supports future upgrades without replacing cable

• DWDM technology: terabits per second

2. EMI Immunity

• Complete immunity to EMI/RFI

• Glass doesn't conduct electricity

• No crosstalk between fibers

• Works near power lines, motors, generators

3. Low Attenuation

• 0.2 dB/km (single-mode)

• Copper: 20+ dB/km

• Longer distances without repeaters

• 100+ km links possible

4. Enhanced Security

• Extremely difficult to tap

• No electromagnetic emissions

• Tapping requires physical fiber access

• Ideal for classified/military networks

5. Lightweight & Small

• Much thinner than copper

• 125 μm cladding (hair-thin)

• Easy to install in tight spaces

• Less weight in cable trays

6. Safety & Durability

• No spark risk (non-conductive)

• Corrosion resistant

• No ground loops

• LSZH jackets for safety

Fiber Optic Disadvantages

Higher Cost

• Cable more expensive than copper

• Specialized connectors (SC, LC, ST)

• Fusion splicer costs $10,000+

Specialized Installation

• Requires trained technicians

• Precision alignment needed

• Bending radius must be maintained

Fragile

• Glass core can break

• Not suitable for harsh physical environments

• Requires careful handling

⚠ Fiber is NOT affected by EMI but IS affected by bending. Tight bends cause light to escape (macro/micro bending losses). Always follow minimum bend radius specifications.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Critical Angle Calculation

Problem: A fiber has core refractive index n₁ = 1.48 and cladding refractive index n₂ = 1.46. Calculate the critical angle.

Solution:

Step 1: Identify given values:

    n₁ = 1.48 (core)

    n₂ = 1.46 (cladding)

Step 2: Apply critical angle formula:

θc = arcsin(n₂ / n₁)
θc = arcsin(1.46 / 1.48)
θc = arcsin(0.9865)
θc = 80.63°

Step 3: Interpretation:

• Any light striking the core-cladding boundary at an angle > 80.63° will undergo TIR

• Light at angles < 80.63° will refract into the cladding and be lost

Example 2: Numerical Aperture Calculation

Problem: A fiber has n₁ = 1.48 and n₂ = 1.46. Calculate the Numerical Aperture (NA) and acceptance angle.

Solution:

Step 1: Apply NA formula:

NA = √(n₁² - n₂²)
NA = √(1.48² - 1.46²)
NA = √(2.1904 - 2.1316)
NA = √(0.0588)
NA = 0.2425

Step 2: Calculate acceptance angle:

θa = arcsin(NA)
θa = arcsin(0.2425)
θa = 14.04°

Step 3: Interpretation:

• Light cone half-angle = 14.04°

• Full acceptance cone = 2 × 14.04° = 28.08°

• Light within this cone is guided; outside it escapes

Example 3: Snell's Law Verification

Problem: Light traveling in core (n₁ = 1.48) strikes the boundary at 85°. Does TIR occur? Calculate the refraction angle if TIR does NOT occur.

Solution:

Step 1: Compare with critical angle:

    From Example 1, θc = 80.63°

    Given θ₁ = 85° > θc = 80.63°

TIR Check:
85° > 80.63° → TIR OCCURS

Step 2: Since TIR occurs, no refraction happens — light reflects back into the core.

Step 3: What if θ₁ = 75°? (Below critical angle)

Snell's Law:
n₁ × sin(θ₁) = n₂ × sin(θ₂)
1.48 × sin(75°) = 1.46 × sin(θ₂)
1.48 × 0.9659 = 1.46 × sin(θ₂)
1.4296 = 1.46 × sin(θ₂)
sin(θ₂) = 0.9792
θ₂ = 78.3°

• Light refracts into cladding at 78.3° — signal is LOST!

Example 4: Relative Refractive Index Difference

Problem: A fiber has n₁ = 1.48 and n₂ = 1.46. Calculate the relative refractive index difference (Δ).

Solution:

Step 1: Apply formula (exact):

Δ = (n₁² - n₂²) / (2 × n₁²)
Δ = (1.48² - 1.46²) / (2 × 1.48²)
Δ = (2.1904 - 2.1316) / (2 × 2.1904)
Δ = 0.0588 / 4.3808
Δ = 0.0134 (1.34%)

Step 2: Verify using approximate formula:

Δ ≈ (n₁ - n₂) / n₁
Δ ≈ (1.48 - 1.46) / 1.48
Δ ≈ 0.02 / 1.48
Δ ≈ 0.0135 (1.35%)

Both formulas give approximately the same result. Typical Δ = 1-3% for standard fibers.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1

Confusing n₁ and n₂

n₁ = core (higher)

n₂ = cladding (lower)

Mistake 2

Wrong angle units

Use degrees for final answer

Use radians in calculator

Mistake 3

Forgetting arc vs arc

θc = arcsin(n₂/n₁)

NOT sin(n₂/n₁)

Mistake 4

Confusing NA with acceptance angle

NA = sin(θa)

θa = arcsin(NA)

⚠ Exam Tip: When given refractive indices, always verify n₁ > n₂ first. Then apply formulas in order: Critical Angle → NA → Acceptance Angle.
