COLLISION IN NETWORKS

Physical Layer — Complete Guide for Exam

What is a Collision?

A collision occurs when two or more signals overlap on the same shared medium at the same time, causing mutual interference. The resulting corrupted signal cannot be correctly interpreted by any receiver.

Definition:
Collision = simultaneous transmission on shared channel → corrupted data
When two devices transmit at the same time, their electrical signals combine. The receiver sees garbled data that matches neither original signal.
⚠ Collisions are NOT errors in the signal itself — they are a consequence of the medium-access method. Networks that use shared media (half-duplex) are inherently prone to collisions.

Why Do Collisions Happen?

Shared Medium

Multiple devices connected to the same wire or frequency band compete for access.

Propagation Delay

Signals take time to travel. A device may not detect another's transmission until it's too late.

No Central Coordination

In distributed networks, no single controller decides who transmits when.

Half-Duplex Operation

Devices can transmit or receive but not both simultaneously on the same channel.

Collision Domain vs Broadcast Domain

Understanding these two domains is critical for network design:

Collision Domain

A group of devices where collisions can occur between any two devices.

Each port on a switch = separate collision domain

All ports on a hub = ONE collision domain

Broadcast Domain

A group of devices where a broadcast frame reaches all devices.

Each VLAN = separate broadcast domain

Switches forward broadcasts to all ports

DeviceCollision DomainBroadcast Domain
Hub1 (all ports share)1 (same as collision)
SwitchPer port (isolated)1 (all ports, unless VLANs)
RouterPer port (isolated)Per port (separate)
⚠ A switch with 24 ports creates 24 separate collision domains but 1 broadcast domain (unless VLANs are configured). This is why switches dramatically reduce collisions compared to hubs.

Key Concepts

Slot Time

The minimum time a device must wait after transmitting before it can assume its frame was not involved in a collision. For Ethernet: 51.2 μs at 10 Mbps.

Critical Distance

Maximum cable length where collision detection works. If the round-trip delay exceeds the slot time, collisions go undetected.

Runt Frame

A frame smaller than the minimum 64 bytes, typically caused by a collision detected mid-transmission. Always discarded.

Jam Signal

A 32-bit signal sent by a station after detecting a collision to ensure all other stations also detect it.

CSMA/CD — Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Detection

CSMA/CD is the medium access control method used in classic Ethernet (IEEE 802.3). It allows stations to detect and recover from collisions on shared media.

Protocol Name Breakdown:
CS = Carrier Sense | MA = Multiple Access | CD = Collision Detection
CS: Listen before transmitting. MA: Multiple devices share the medium. CD: Detect collisions during transmission.

CSMA/CD Algorithm

ALGORITHM CSMA/CD
// Station wants to transmit a frame
1. LISTEN to the medium (Carrier Sense)
    IF medium is idle:
        BEGIN TRANSMISSION
    ELSE:
        WAIT until medium is idle

2. TRANSMIT while monitoring the medium
    IF collision detected (voltage spike):
        STOP transmission immediately
        SEND 32-bit jam signal
        GOTO step 3

3. RANDOM BACKOFF (Binary Exponential Backoff)
    Pick random wait time from [0, 2^k - 1] × slot time
    k = min(attempt number, 10)

4. REPEAT from step 1
    IF 16 attempts fail: ABORT transmission

Binary Exponential Backoff (BEB)

BEB is the algorithm that determines how long a station waits after a collision before retrying:

Backoff Time Formula:
T_backoff = random(0, 2^k - 1) × slot_time
Where k = min(n, 10), n = number of collision attempts. slot_time = 51.2 μs for 10 Mbps Ethernet.

CSMA/CD Step-by-Step Timeline

Collision Detection Timeline

T₀: Station A senses medium → idle → begins transmitting

T₁: Station B senses medium → still sees idle (propagation delay) → begins transmitting

T₂: Signals collide! Both detect voltage anomaly on the wire

T₃: Both stations stop transmitting, send jam signal

T₄: Both stations run binary exponential backoff

T₅: One station wins, retransmits successfully

Slot Time Calculation

Slot Time (Ethernet 10 Mbps):
Slot Time = 2 × T_propagation + T_jam
Must be large enough to detect a collision from the farthest station. For 10 Mbps Ethernet: 51.2 μs = 512 bit times.
Maximum Cable Length:
Max Length = (Slot Time × Speed of Signal) / 2
For 10 Mbps Ethernet with cable velocity ~0.7c: Max ≈ 2500m (accounting for repeater delays).
⚠ This is why Ethernet has a maximum cable length. If the cable is too long, a station could finish transmitting before the collision signal reaches it — causing undetected collisions.

Efficiency of CSMA/CD

Channel Utilization:
Efficiency = 1 / (1 + 5a)
Where a = T_propagation / T_transmission. As propagation delay increases relative to transmission time, efficiency drops.

For short cables and large frames, CSMA/CD is very efficient. For long cables with small frames, efficiency degrades significantly.

CSMA/CA — Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance

CSMA/CA is used in Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11) because wireless stations cannot reliably detect collisions. Instead of detecting, CSMA/CA tries to avoid collisions altogether.

Why CA instead of CD for Wi-Fi?
Wireless radios cannot transmit and receive simultaneously on the same frequency
Also, the "hidden terminal problem" means a station may not hear another's transmission. Detection is unreliable, so avoidance is used instead.
⚠ CSMA/CA does NOT eliminate collisions — it reduces their probability. Collisions can still happen in Wi-Fi, especially with hidden terminals.

CSMA/CA Algorithm

ALGORITHM CSMA/CA
// Station wants to transmit a frame
1. LISTEN to the medium (Carrier Sense)
    IF medium is idle for DIFS time:
        GOTO step 3
    ELSE:
        GOTO step 2

2. DEFER and wait
    CW (Contention Window) starts at CW_min
    Wait random time in [0, CW] × slot time
    After medium idle for DIFS: countdown begins

3. SEND RTS (optional, for large frames)
    Receiver responds with CTS after SIFS
    All stations hearing RTS/CTS SET NAV (defer)

4. TRANSMIT frame

5. WAIT for ACK from receiver
    IF ACK received: SUCCESS, reset CW to CW_min
    ELSE: CW = min(CW × 2, CW_max), retry

CSMA/CA Timing Intervals

DIFS

DCF Inter-Frame Spacing

Time the medium must be idle before a station can attempt transmission. Highest-priority traffic uses shorter IFS.

SIFS

Short Inter-Frame Spacing

Shortest interval, used for ACKs and CTS. Gives priority to control frames over data frames.

EIFS

Extended Inter-Frame Spacing

Used after error/damaged frame detection. Longer wait reduces chance of collision with retransmissions.

NAV

Network Allocation Vector

A timer set by RTS/CTS. All stations hearing RTS/CTS set their NAV and defer transmission until NAV expires.

IFS Priority Order:
SIFS < DIFS < EIFS
Shorter IFS = higher priority. ACKs use SIFS, regular data uses DIFS. This ensures ACKs always get through first.

Hidden Terminal Problem

The hidden terminal problem is a major challenge in wireless networks that CSMA/CA attempts to address with RTS/CTS:

Hidden Terminal Scenario

Stations A and C are both in range of B, but A and C cannot hear each other.

1. A transmits to B — C cannot hear A's signal

2. C senses medium → appears idle → transmits to B

3. COLLISION at B! — Both signals overlap at B

4. Neither A nor C knows about the collision

RTS/CTS Solution:
A → RTS → B → CTS → All (including C)
When A sends RTS and B responds with CTS, station C hears the CTS and knows to remain silent for the duration. This reserves the channel.
⚠ RTS/CTS adds overhead. For small frames, it's more efficient to send without RTS/CTS. Threshold: use RTS/CTS when frame size > RTS threshold (typically 256-512 bytes).

Exposed Terminal Problem

The opposite of hidden terminal: a station unnecessarily defers because it hears a transmission that wouldn't actually interfere with its own communication.

Exposed Terminal Scenario

Station B is transmitting to A. Station C wants to transmit to D (in the opposite direction).

C hears B's transmission → defers. But C→D transmission wouldn't interfere with B→A!

Result: unnecessary reduction in throughput

Contention Window

Exponential Backoff (Wi-Fi):
Wait = random(0, 2^n - 1) × slot_time
Where n starts at 0 and increases with each failed attempt. CW doubles each time: CW_min → 2×CW_min → 4×CW_min ... up to CW_max.

Worked Examples

Example 1: CSMA/CD — Binary Exponential Backoff Calculation

Problem: A 10 Mbps Ethernet station experiences its 4th collision. What are the possible backoff times?

Solution:

Step 1: Determine k = min(n, 10) = min(4, 10) = 4
Step 2: Backoff range = [0, 2^4 - 1] = [0, 15] → 16 possible values
Step 3: Multiply by slot time: T = random(0,15) × 51.2 μs
Possible backoff times:
0, 51.2, 102.4, 153.6, ..., 768 μs
16 evenly-spaced values from 0 to 768 μs. Average wait ≈ 384 μs.
Example 2: CSMA/CD — Maximum Collisions Before Abort

Problem: After how many consecutive collisions does CSMA/CD give up? What happens?

Solution:

Step 1: CSMA/CD allows a maximum of 16 collisions
Step 2: After 16 failures, the station ABORTS the frame
Step 3: Error is reported to the upper layer (LLC/network layer)
Step 4: The frame is DISCARDED — it is NOT retransmitted at the MAC layer
Max wait at 16th collision:
T_max = 1023 × 51.2 μs = 52.38 ms
With k capped at 10, the max backoff range stays [0, 1023]. The probability of collision decreases with each retry as more time slots are available.
Example 3: CSMA/CA — RTS/CTS Handshake Timing

Problem: A Wi-Fi station sends a 1500-byte frame using RTS/CTS. Calculate the overhead if SIFS = 10 μs, DIFS = 50 μs, and slot time = 20 μs. Assume 11 Mbps data rate and 1 Mbps control rate.

Solution:

Step 1: RTS frame = 20 bytes at 1 Mbps = 20 × 8 / 1,000,000 = 160 μs
Step 2: SIFS wait = 10 μs
Step 3: CTS frame = 14 bytes at 1 Mbps = 14 × 8 / 1,000,000 = 112 μs
Step 4: SIFS wait = 10 μs
Step 5: DATA frame = 1500 bytes at 11 Mbps = 1500 × 8 / 11,000,000 ≈ 1091 μs
Step 6: SIFS wait = 10 μs
Step 7: ACK frame = 14 bytes at 1 Mbps = 112 μs
Total overhead:
RTS + SIFS + CTS + SIFS + SIFS + ACK = 160 + 10 + 112 + 10 + 10 + 112 = 414 μs
Plus DIFS = 50 μs before RTS. Total overhead ≈ 464 μs. Data transmission = 1091 μs. Efficiency ≈ 70%.
Example 4: Collision Domain Identification

Problem: A network has: 1 hub with 8 ports, connected to 1 switch port. The switch has 24 ports. How many collision domains exist?

Solution:

Step 1: The hub creates 1 collision domain for all 8 of its connected devices
Step 2: The switch isolates each port into its own collision domain
Step 3: One switch port connects to the hub → that port + hub = 1 collision domain
Step 4: The remaining 23 switch ports each form their own collision domain
Total collision domains:
1 (hub group) + 23 (remaining switch ports) = 24 collision domains
Without the hub, it would be 24 separate collision domains (one per switch port).

Problem: Trace the contention window values for a station that experiences 5 consecutive collisions on 802.11a (CW_min=15, CW_max=1023).

Solution:

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1

Using CSMA/CD for Wi-Fi

Wi-Fi uses CSMA/CA, not CD

Mistake 2

Confusing CW_min with CW_max

Start at CW_min, double until CW_max

Mistake 3

Forgetting k is capped at 10

k = min(n, 10) in CSMA/CD

Mistake 4

Assuming CSMA/CA prevents all collisions

It only reduces probability
