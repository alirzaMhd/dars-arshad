NOISE IN DATA TRANSMISSION

Physical Layer — Complete Guide for Exam

What is Noise?

Noise is unwanted electrical or electromagnetic energy that interferes with a signal and degrades its quality during transmission. It is the single most important factor that limits the performance of communication systems.

Definition:
Noise = any unwanted signal that corrupts the desired signal
Noise can originate from external sources (interference) or from within the system itself (thermal noise).
⚠ Noise is inevitable in all real communication systems. The goal is not to eliminate noise entirely but to minimize its impact on signal quality.

Why Noise Matters

Signal Degradation

Noise distorts the original signal, making it harder to interpret at the receiver end.

Data Errors

Noise can cause bits to flip (0→1 or 1→0), resulting in corrupted data.

Reduced Range

Higher noise levels limit how far a signal can travel before becoming unreadable.

Lower Throughput

More noise means more retransmissions, reducing effective data rate.

Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)

SNR is the most important metric for measuring noise impact:

SNR (linear):
SNR = Signal Power / Noise Power
Higher SNR means better signal quality. SNR of 100 means signal is 100× stronger than noise.
SNR in Decibels:
SNR(dB) = 10 × log₁₀(Signal Power / Noise Power)
SNR in dB is more commonly used. Typical values: 20-40 dB for wired, 10-25 dB for wireless.
SNR and Channel Capacity (Shannon-Hartley):
C = B × log₂(1 + SNR)
Where C = channel capacity (bps), B = bandwidth (Hz), SNR = signal-to-noise ratio (linear).

Classification of Noise

By Source

• Internal (within the system)

• External (from environment)

By Behavior

• Random (statistical, predictable)

• Impulse (sudden, unpredictable)

By Type

• Thermal (Johnson-Nyquist)

• EMI (Electromagnetic)

• Crosstalk (Signal coupling)

• Impulse (Bursts)

Thermal Noise (Johnson-Nyquist Noise)

Thermal noise is the most fundamental type of noise, caused by the random thermal motion of electrons in a conductor. It exists in ALL conductors and cannot be eliminated.

Johnson-Nyquist Formula:
P_noise = k × T × B
Where: P_noise = thermal noise power (watts), k = Boltzmann's constant (1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K), T = absolute temperature (Kelvin), B = bandwidth (Hz)
⚠ Thermal noise is always present at temperatures above absolute zero (0 K = -273.15°C). Even at room temperature, it causes measurable noise.

Boltzmann's Constant

k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K (Joules per Kelvin)
This is a universal physical constant relating thermal energy to temperature. Memorize this value for exams.

Temperature Conversion

For thermal noise calculations, temperature must be in Kelvin:

Celsius to Kelvin:
T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15

Room Temperature

20°C = 293.15 K

Standard Temperature

25°C = 298.15 K

Freezing Point

0°C = 273.15 K

Boiling Point

100°C = 373.15 K

Noise Power Density

Thermal noise power per unit bandwidth:
N₀ = k × T (watts/Hz)
At room temperature (293 K): N₀ ≈ 4.0 × 10⁻²¹ W/Hz ≈ -174 dBm/Hz

Key Properties of Thermal Noise

Uniform Spectrum

Thermal noise has equal power across all frequencies (white noise).

Gaussian Distribution

Noise voltage follows a Gaussian (normal) probability distribution.

Temperature Dependent

Higher temperature = more thermal energy = more noise.

Bandwidth Dependent

Wider bandwidth captures more noise power.

Noise Figure and Noise Factor

Noise Factor (F):
F = SNR_input / SNR_output
Ratio of input SNR to output SNR. F ≥ 1 (always degrades or maintains SNR).
Noise Figure (NF):
NF(dB) = 10 × log₁₀(F)
Noise figure in decibels. Ideal amplifier: NF = 0 dB (F = 1). Typical amplifiers: 1-10 dB.

Electromagnetic Interference (EMI)

EMI is unwanted electromagnetic energy from external sources that couples into communication lines and corrupts signals.

Sources of EMI

Power Lines

50/60 Hz hum from electrical power distribution and high-voltage transmission lines.

Motors & Machinery

Electric motors, transformers, and industrial equipment generate broadband EMI.

Radio Transmitters

Cell phones, Wi-Fi routers, radio/TV stations create intentional and unintentional EMI.

Lightning

Natural lightning strikes produce powerful EMI bursts over wide areas.

Effects on Communication

⚠ EMI can cause permanent damage to sensitive electronics, not just signal degradation. Proper shielding is essential for system protection.

Crosstalk

Crosstalk is unwanted coupling of signals between adjacent communication channels or wires.

Types of Crosstalk

Near-End Crosstalk (NEXT)

Interference measured at the same end as the transmitter. Occurs when signal from one wire couples to adjacent wire.

Far-End Crosstalk (FEXT)

Interference measured at the opposite end from the transmitter. Signal couples along the length of the cable.

Sources of Crosstalk

Telephone Lines

Adjacent copper pairs in a cable bundle can pick up each other's signals.

Ethernet Cables

Poorly twisted pairs or damaged cables increase crosstalk between wires.

Circuit Board Traces

Parallel traces on PCBs can couple signals at high frequencies.

Fiber Optics

Even fiber can experience crosstalk at splitters and connectors.

Impulse Noise

Impulse noise consists of sudden, short-duration bursts of high-amplitude noise. Unlike thermal noise, it is non-continuous and unpredictable.

Characteristics

Burst Duration

Typically microseconds to milliseconds.

High Amplitude

Can be much stronger than the signal itself.

Non-Gaussian

Does not follow normal distribution like thermal noise.

Unpredictable

Cannot be modeled statistically as easily as thermal noise.

Sources of Impulse Noise

Lightning

Atmospheric discharges create powerful electromagnetic pulses.

Power Switching

Turning on/off motors, relays, and switches generates bursts.

Electrical Arcs

Welding equipment and power line arcing create impulse noise.

Cosmic Radiation

Space-originating radiation causes random bit flips in electronics.

Other Noise Types

Quantization Noise

Error introduced when analog signals are digitized. More bits = less quantization noise.

Shot Noise

Caused by discrete nature of electric charge. Dominant in photodetectors and semiconductors.

Phase Noise

Random fluctuations in oscillator frequency. Degrades modulation quality.

Intermodulation Noise

Nonlinear mixing of signals creating unwanted frequency components.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Basic Thermal Noise Calculation

Problem: Calculate the thermal noise power in a 10 MHz bandwidth at room temperature (20°C).

Solution:

Step 1: Convert temperature to Kelvin:

T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15
T = 20 + 273.15 = 293.15 K

Step 2: Identify all values:

    k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K

    T = 293.15 K

    B = 10 MHz = 10 × 10⁶ Hz

Step 3: Apply Johnson-Nyquist formula:

P_noise = k × T × B
P_noise = (1.38 × 10⁻²³) × 293.15 × (10 × 10⁶)
P_noise = 4.045 × 10⁻¹⁴ watts

Step 4: Convert to dBm:

P(dBm) = 10 × log₁₀(P / 1mW)
P(dBm) = 10 × log₁₀(4.045 × 10⁻¹⁴ / 10⁻³) = -103.93 dBm
Example 2: SNR Calculation

Problem: A receiver has a signal power of 5 mW and noise power of 0.02 mW. Calculate SNR in linear and dB.

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate linear SNR:

SNR = Signal Power / Noise Power
SNR = 5 / 0.02 = 250 (linear)

Step 2: Convert to decibels:

SNR(dB) = 10 × log₁₀(SNR)
SNR(dB) = 10 × log₁₀(250) = 23.98 dB ≈ 24 dB

Interpretation: Signal is 250× stronger than noise. 24 dB SNR is good for most applications.

Example 3: Effect of Temperature on Noise

Problem: Compare thermal noise power at 20°C vs 50°C for a 1 MHz bandwidth. How much does noise increase?

Solution:

Step 1: Convert temperatures to Kelvin:

    T₁ = 20 + 273.15 = 293.15 K

    T₂ = 50 + 273.15 = 323.15 K

Step 2: Calculate noise power for each:

At 20°C:
P₁ = (1.38 × 10⁻²³) × 293.15 × 10⁶ = 4.045 × 10⁻¹⁸ W
At 50°C:
P₂ = (1.38 × 10⁻²³) × 323.15 × 10⁶ = 4.459 × 10⁻¹⁸ W

Step 3: Calculate percentage increase:

Increase = (P₂ - P₁) / P₁ × 100%
Increase = (4.459 - 4.045) / 4.045 × 100% = 10.23% increase

A 30°C temperature rise caused approximately 10% more thermal noise.

Example 4: Shannon Capacity with Noise

Problem: A channel has 5 MHz bandwidth and SNR of 30 dB. What is the maximum data rate?

Solution:

Step 1: Convert SNR from dB to linear:

SNR_linear = 10^(SNR_dB / 10)
SNR_linear = 10^(30/10) = 10³ = 1000

Step 2: Apply Shannon-Hartley theorem:

C = B × log₂(1 + SNR)
C = 5 × 10⁶ × log₂(1 + 1000)
C = 5 × 10⁶ × log₂(1001)
C = 5 × 10⁶ × 9.97
C ≈ 49.85 Mbps

This is the theoretical maximum. Practical systems achieve 60-80% of Shannon capacity.

Example 5: Noise Figure and Amplifier Performance

Problem: An amplifier has a noise figure of 3 dB. If the input SNR is 30 dB, what is the output SNR?

Solution:

Step 1: Convert noise figure to noise factor:

F = 10^(NF/10)
F = 10^(3/10) = 2.0

Step 2: Apply noise factor definition:

F = SNR_input / SNR_output
2.0 = SNR_input / SNR_output
SNR_output = SNR_input / 2.0

Step 3: Convert input SNR to linear:

SNR_input (linear) = 10^(30/10) = 1000
SNR_output = 1000 / 2.0 = 500 (linear)

Step 4: Convert output SNR to dB:

SNR_output(dB) = 10 × log₁₀(500)
SNR_output(dB) = 26.99 dB ≈ 27 dB

The amplifier reduced SNR from 30 dB to 27 dB due to its 3 dB noise figure.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1

Using Celsius instead of Kelvin

Always add 273.15

Mistake 2

Confusing noise factor with noise figure

Factor = linear, Figure = dB

Mistake 3

Forgetting to convert SNR dB to linear

Shannon formula needs linear SNR

Mistake 4

Assuming noise can be eliminated

Thermal noise is fundamental
